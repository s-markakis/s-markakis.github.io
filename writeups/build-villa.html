This is part 6 of the Build series, and the one closest to everyday installation work in Crete. Most of the island's rental villas sit where fibre doesn't reach and VDSL tops out at a few Mb/s, so Starlink has become the default. A villa network looks like a home network, but it has a business's problems: paying guests who judge the stay by the Wi-Fi, an owner whose devices and smart home must stay private, and no IT person within an hour's drive.

    
The design leans on three ideas: treat each booking as one trusted group but never trust the next one, manage everything remotely through a tunnel the villa dials out itself, and make every component recover from a power cut without anyone touching it.

The Brief

  
A fictional but very typical Cretan villa: rented weekly from May to October, used by the owner's family a few weeks a year, empty in winter.


  
The main house has a living area, kitchen and four bedrooms on two floors, each bedroom with a smart TV. The pool and garden are where guests spend the day, phones in hand. A guest annex with its own bedroom sits 40 m down the garden. The owner keeps a locked office with a PC and a NAS of family photos. The smart home runs the alarm, smart locks (guests get door codes per booking), pool controller, irrigation and gate. Four exterior cameras cover the gate, driveway and pool area. Internet is Starlink; the only alternative is mobile. The installer manages it all from Heraklion.

02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Each one is a trade-off between what a villa is and what a network textbook assumes.


  
1 · One booking is one trust group

  
A café isolates every guest device from every other one. A villa is rented by one family or one group of friends at a time, and they want to cast from their phones to the TVs. So the guest VLAN has no client isolation; the TVs sit in it on purpose. The isolation that matters is between bookings, and that's handled by changing the Wi-Fi key at every checkout (step 8).


  
2 · Starlink primary, 4G backup

  
Starlink gives 150–250 Mb/s down here; the 4G signal on the hill gives 20–40. The roles follow the numbers. The backup carries guests (shaped) but refuses camera and smart-home cloud uploads, which would only compete with the guests for a small pipe.


  
3 · A 60 GHz bridge instead of a trench

  
The annex is 40 m away across a garden with a stone wall. Trenching for a cable costs more than the whole network and wrecks the garden. A pair of wAP 60G radios gives a gigabit-class, line-of-sight bridge that carries the VLAN trunk. At 40 m, rain fade is irrelevant; the only enemy is a growing tree, which the annual service visit checks.


  
4 · CAKE instead of hand-made priorities

  
The other Build posts use queue trees with explicit classes. That works when the line rate is fixed. Starlink's isn't, and a villa has no IT person to retune queues. CAKE (a queue discipline built into RouterOS 7) shares bandwidth fairly per host, keeps latency low for small flows, and with its NAT mode it sees the real inside hosts behind masquerade. One queue per direction, set slightly under Starlink's typical evening capacity.

    mDNS repeating goes both ways
    
Repeating mDNS between the owner and guest VLANs means guests' phones can also see the names of the owner's devices that advertise themselves (a NAS, a printer). They still can't connect, because there's no rule from VLAN 20 to VLAN 10, but the names leak. If that bothers the owner, leave the repeater off and let the owner cast from a device that joins VILLA-Guest during family stays.

ip access-list standard NAT-ALL
 permit 10.70.0.0 0.0.255.255
ip nat inside source list NAT-ALL interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0 overload
/interface list member add list=WAN interface=ether1
/ip route
add dst-address=1.1.1.1/32 gateway=100.64.0.1 scope=10 comment="probe via starlink"
add dst-address=0.0.0.0/0 gateway=1.1.1.1 target-scope=11 check-gateway=ping distance=1 comment="main: starlink"
add dst-address=192.168.100.1/32 gateway=ether1 comment="dish status page"
/ip dhcp-client add interface=ether1 add-default-route=no use-peer-dns=no comment="Starlink (bypass)" script={
  :if ($bound=1) do={
    /ip route set [find comment="probe via starlink"] gateway=$"gateway-address"
  }
}
/ip firewall nat add chain=srcnat action=masquerade out-interface-list=WAN


  
In bypass mode the Starlink router only powers the dish and hands its DHCP lease straight to R1. Starlink's gateway address can change after a reconnect, so the probe route can't be static. The DHCP client's script rewrites it at every lease. That's the same technique as in the Starlink failover post, which covers it in more depth. The extra route to 192.168.100.1 keeps the dish's status page reachable from the owner's and operator's devices, which is the quickest way to see obstruction and outage history remotely.


  

    Carrier NAT and IPv6
    
Starlink residential addresses are in 100.64.0.0/10: carrier-grade NAT, no inbound connections, ever. Nothing in this design needs inbound (step 9 dials out). Starlink also hands out IPv6 prefixes; this post keeps the villa IPv4-only for simplicity. If you add IPv6, remember that every firewall rule above needs an IPv6 twin, or guests on IPv6 bypass the matrix entirely (see IPv6 on RouterOS).

  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • /ip dhcp-client print: bound, 100.64.x.x; /ip route print where comment~"probe" shows the same gateway.
  • Guests browse; a speed test on VILLA-Guest gives Starlink-class numbers.
  • From the owner's laptop, http://192.168.100.1 shows the dish's status.

CAKE: Fair and Fast on a Moving Target

  
One queue per direction that keeps video calls smooth while someone downloads, without anyone tuning classes.


  

    
  


  
R1 — CAKE on the Starlink link
! R1 — no CAKE on IOS: a shaper with fair-queue gives per-FLOW fairness,
! not per-host fairness behind NAT. Set it just under typical evening capacity.
policy-map STARLINK-CHILD
 class class-default
  fair-queue
policy-map STARLINK-OUT
 class class-default
  shape average 20000000
  service-policy STARLINK-CHILD
interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0
 service-policy output STARLINK-OUT
/queue type
add name=cake-up kind=cake cake-diffserv=diffserv4 cake-nat=yes cake-flowmode=dual-srchost
add name=cake-down kind=cake cake-diffserv=diffserv4 cake-flowmode=dual-dsthost
/ip firewall mangle add chain=forward action=mark-packet new-packet-mark=from-starlink \
    in-interface=ether1 passthrough=no
/queue tree
add name=starlink-up parent=ether1 queue=cake-up max-limit=20M
add name=starlink-down parent=global packet-mark=from-starlink queue=cake-down max-limit=180M


  
dual-srchost / dual-dsthost make CAKE fair per host first and per flow second. One guest's twenty download streams don't beat another guest's single video call. cake-nat=yes on the upload side is the important flag: by the time a packet reaches ether1 it has been masqueraded to Starlink's address, and NAT mode lets CAKE look up the original inside host anyway. That's the problem the gaming center had to work around with fq-codel. On the download side, the queue sees the inside address already, because un-NAT happens before routing.


  

    Setting a limit on a link that keeps changing
    
CAKE only controls latency if the bottleneck is in R1, which means the max-limit must be below what Starlink delivers. Too high, and the queue builds in Starlink's equipment where CAKE can't manage it. Too low, and you throw capacity away. Look at the Starlink app's speed history for typical evening throughput (the busiest time), and set roughly 85–90% of that. Revisit it at the annual service visit; capacity in an area changes as satellites and users are added. As elsewhere in this series, no FastTrack: fast-tracked packets would skip CAKE.

  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • One laptop runs a large download; a second runs a video call and a bufferbloat test. The call stays clear and the test grades A or B.
  • /queue tree print stats: drops on starlink-down while the download runs.

A New Guest Key for Every Booking

  
At checkout, the old Wi-Fi key stops working and the manager gets the new one. No one has to remember to do it.


  

    
  


  
R1 — rotate the guest key and send it to the manager
! Cisco has no on-box random key + e-mail. The usual pattern is a small scheduled
! script on the operator's hub that generates the key and pushes one line to the
! wireless controller (over SSH or RESTCONF), then mails the manager:
wlan VILLA-Guest 1 VILLA-Guest

! changing the key re-keys the WLAN and disconnects the previous guests' devices
/tool e-mail set server=smtp.mail.example port=587 tls=starttls user=villa-r1@mail.example password=********
/system script add name=rotate-guest-psk source={
  :local key [:rndstr length=14 from="abcdefghjkmnpqrstuvwxyzABCDEFGHJKMNPQRSTUVWXYZ23456789"]
  /interface wifi security set [find name=sec-guest] passphrase=$key
  /tool e-mail send to="manager@villa.example" subject="VILLA-Guest key for the next booking" \
      body=("Network: VILLA-Guest\nKey: " . $key . "\nPrint it on the welcome card.")
  :log warning "guest Wi-Fi key rotated"
}
/system scheduler add name=checkout-rotate start-date=2026-11-07 start-time=11:00:00 interval=7d \
    on-event=rotate-guest-psk


  
Weekly Saturday-to-Saturday rentals make the schedule trivial. For bookings with other dates, the manager asks the operator to run the script, or a booking-system webhook triggers it through the management tunnel. The character set leaves out look-alikes (0/O, 1/l/I), so the key can be typed from a printed card. The log line records that the key changed, never the key itself.


  

    What changing the key does to devices still connected
    
Changing the security profile makes CAPsMAN re-provision the APs, and every guest device reconnects with the new key or not at all. That's the point at checkout, but it also means: don't run it while guests are still there, and make sure the TVs are wired (they are), so they don't need the new key typed in every week. The smart-home devices sit on their own SSID with its own key, so the rotation never touches them.

  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • Run /system script run rotate-guest-psk: a phone on the old key disconnects and can't rejoin; the manager's mailbox has the new key; the log shows "rotated" without the key.
  • The smart-home devices on VILLA-Home stay connected throughout.

Residual Risk

  
What the network can't do on its own, and what covers each gap.


  
    
    
  • Guests on the guest VLAN can see each other. That's deliberate (one booking, one group). If the villa is ever let room by room to separate parties, turn on client isolation and put each TV on its own small VLAN, or the design's main assumption is gone.
    • 
    
  • Smart locks and the alarm depend on their vendors' clouds. The network keeps guests away from them; the vendors' account security (owner's MFA, per-booking door codes that expire) is what protects the door.
    • 
    
  • The smart TVs remember things. Guests log into streaming apps on them. A "sign out of all apps" step in the changeover checklist (or the TV's guest mode, where available) matters as much as the Wi-Fi key.
    • 
    
  • The owner's office cable. It's in a locked room for a reason. If the owner ever moves the PC to a shared space, that port should move to the guest VLAN.
    • 
    
  • One operator, many villas. The hub's keys open every property it manages. Protect it accordingly: MFA, a dedicated machine, and a separate WireGuard key per villa so one can be revoked alone.
    •

The Attacker's View

  
I test networks like this for a living. Here's what I'd try against a holiday villa full of strangers, and why each one is a decision rather than an oversight.


  
    
    
  • Evil-twin the guest SSID. Standing up a second "VILLA-Guest" from the garden harvests the welcome-card key and lets me MITM guests. It buys nothing of the owner's: guest traffic is VLAN 20, forward-filtered to the internet only, and the smart-home and cameras live on VLANs no guest rule reaches.
    • 
    
  • Reach the cameras or the NVR. CCTV is VLAN 40 with no forward rule from the guest or smart-home VLANs; only the owner's VLAN 10 can open the NVR. From the guest network a scan of the range answers with the gateway and the cast-enabled TVs, and nothing else.
    • 
    
  • Hop out of the guest VLAN. Double-tagging to jump onto VLAN 10 goes nowhere: trunks are admit-only-vlan-tagged, access ports carry a pvid, and the native VLAN is never a data VLAN, so the injected tag is dropped at the first bridge.
    • 
    
  • Spoof a smart-home device onto its SSID. The 2.4 GHz home network is a separate WPA2 key, not the rotating guest key, so a leaked welcome card never touches the locks or the alarm. Those still lean on the vendors' account security, which the network can't provide.
    • 
    
  • Skip the villa and go for the operator's hub. The WireGuard hub holds a key to every property it manages, so the efficient move is to phish the operator, not the dish. The per-villa key and the hub's own MFA are what make that single compromise survivable — not anything in the villa's config.
    •

    What actually changes in the design
    
Two things get worse on Cisco here. There is no CAKE, so fairness is per flow rather than per host, and one guest with many downloads can still crowd out a video call.
