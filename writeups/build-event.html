This is part 7 of the Build series, and the first network that's designed to be thrown away. Every other Build assumes a building, cable runs and years of service. A festival has a field, a generator and four days. That changes what "good design" means: everything is pre-built and tested in the workshop, every link is a radio or a single armoured fibre, every node runs on generator power with a battery to ride through switchovers, and the most important feature of the network is that the gates keep scanning tickets even when it fails.

01

  
The Brief

  
A fictional but realistic mid-sized festival. The organiser's only firm requirement: "the gates and the bars can't stop".


  
Attendees enter through two gates where 12 handheld scanners check QR tickets against the ticketing company's cloud. The food court has 20 vendors with card terminals and tablet tills, and a charging area where people actually sit down with their phones. The main stage streams a live feed, and a press tent has wired desks for journalists. Staff, security and first aid use tablets. There's no fixed infrastructure: power comes from generators, and the site is 300 m across with olive trees in every line of sight.

02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Each one would be wrong in a permanent building and is right in a field.


  
1 · A star of radio links from the NOC

  
Each zone (gates, food court, stage) has one link back to the NOC tent: 60 GHz radios where there's line of sight over the trees, an armoured fibre to the food court because it's close and carries the most users. A star, not a chain, means a failed link takes out one zone, and the NOC sees exactly which one. 60 GHz is chosen because it's fast, ignores the 5 GHz Wi-Fi congestion, and works fine at festival distances in dry summer weather. Every link is surveyed on Monday and re-aimed if a stage truck ends up in the path.


  
2 · Wi-Fi only where it pays off

  
Covering 3,000 people across an open field with Wi-Fi would mean dozens of APs fighting for airtime. Instead, the public SSID exists only where people sit still (the food court and charging area), and phones elsewhere use mobile data. That keeps the public network to a handful of APs that can be properly planned for density.


  
3 · Three lines, three jobs

  
Two Starlinks and one 5G line, each with a role. Starlink A carries the critical zones and the (shaped) public. Starlink B belongs to the live stream alone. 5G is the backup for the critical zones only. Routing tables, not firewall rules, decide which line each zone may use, so "the public never touches 5G" is simply true: their table has no route there.


  
4 · Configuration is generated, not typed

  
Ten nodes configured by hand in a tent is ten chances for a typo at 07:00 on opening day. Every node's config is generated from one template in the workshop, tested on the bench as a complete network, labelled, and packed. On site, nobody types a config.

The build

  
From the NOC Tent Outwards

  
Steps 1–5 happen twice: first on a workbench in the workshop, then in the field. Every checkpoint is run in both places.

  
Ten steps: the NOC, the backhaul links, the zone devices, addressing, Wi-Fi, the firewall, the three internet lines, public limits, monitoring, and teardown. Backhaul: Radios, Fibre, Zone Switches

  
Three zones, three links back to the NOC. A link failure costs one zone, never the site.


  
▲ Show step 2 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  

    
  


  

    
LinkHardware · notes

    
      
NOC → gates (180 m)wAP 60G pair on 3 m masts, aimed over the olive canopy. Transparent bridge for the trunk (as in the villa). Survey on Monday, re-check after the stage trucks arrive.

      
NOC → food court (90 m)Armoured outdoor fibre, buried 10 cm or run along a fence, SFP+ at both ends. The busiest zone gets the link that can't be blocked by a truck.

      
NOC → stage (240 m)wAP 60G pair. A spare pair and two 5 GHz point-to-point radios are in the NOC case as a quick replacement if a 60 GHz path gets blocked for good.

      
Zone switchesnetPower 16P: outdoor, 16 PoE-out gigabit ports and SFP+, mounted in a ventilated box at the foot of each mast. Each powers its APs and the radio.

    
  


  

    60 GHz and a field full of people
    
A 60 GHz beam is narrow, and anything solid in it (a truck, a tent roof, a crowd standing on a ridge) blocks it. Mount the radios high, aim over the trees and people rather than through them, and plan the mast positions with the production manager, who knows where the trucks will park. Rain at these distances is a non-issue; dust on the radomes is wiped off every morning.

  


  

    Checkpoint: on the bench, then in the field
    
  • /interface w60g monitor wlan60-1 on each radio: connected, rate in the Gb/s range, signal stable over ten minutes.
  • NOC-SW sees all three zone switches on VLAN 99; a 1 GB copy from the NOC laptop to each zone switch's neighbour runs at near line rate.

08 · Step 4 of 10

  
Addressing a Crowd

  
The public VLAN gets a /21 and 15-minute leases. Everything else is small and boring.


  
R1 — DHCP and DNS sized for thousands of phones
/ip address add address=10.80.48.1/21 interface=v50-public
/ip pool add name=p-public ranges=10.80.48.10-10.80.55.250
/ip dhcp-server add name=d-public interface=v50-public address-pool=p-public lease-time=15m
/ip dhcp-server network add address=10.80.48.0/21 gateway=10.80.48.1 dns-server=10.80.48.1
# critical zones: small pools, reservations for every scanner and known till
/ip dhcp-server add name=d-ticket interface=v20-ticket address-pool=p-ticket lease-time=12h
/ip dhcp-server lease add server=d-ticket mac-address=00:24:2B:00:00:01 address=10.80.20.41 comment="scanner G-A-01"
# … the other zones the same way
/ip dns set allow-remote-requests=yes servers=208.67.222.222,208.67.220.220 cache-size=8192KiB \
    max-concurrent-queries=500


  
The arithmetic: about 3,000 people a day, perhaps a third of them on the public Wi-Fi at some point, and phones that come and go every few minutes. With 2-hour leases, a busy afternoon would leave hundreds of addresses held by phones that left long ago. Fifteen-minute leases recycle them. A /21 (about 2,000 addresses) gives headroom on top. DNS gets a bigger cache and more concurrent queries, because thousands of phones all looking up the same few apps is exactly what a cache is for. The resolvers aren't 1.1.1.1 or 9.9.9.9, because those become the probe addresses in step 7.


  

    Checkpoint: on the bench, then in the field
    
  • A DHCP load tester (or 50 VMs) on VLAN 50 all get leases within seconds; /ip dhcp-server lease print count-only where server=d-public matches.
  • Each scanner gets its reserved address.

Public Wi-Fi uses 20 MHz channels: in a dense crowd, more non-overlapping channels beat wider ones. The scanners' SSID only exists at the gates, so a scanner carried off to the bar doesn't wander onto a weak signal and slow down the line. Vendors who brought Wi-Fi-only tills join EVT-PAY with a key handed out at check-in. Wired tills are preferred and get the FOOD-SW ports.


  
No backhaul is perfect for four days. The scanners handle that themselves: they download the ticket list before the gates open, validate online when they can, and fall back to the local list when they can't.

    Twenty vendors, twenty acquirers
    
In the coffee shop, the payment VLAN could only reach one acquirer's host names. At a festival, every vendor arrives with their own terminal from their own bank, and nobody has the full list of host names until Thursday morning. So the payment VLAN gets HTTPS out to anything, and DNS from R1, but nothing inbound and nothing inside the site. That still keeps every till away from three thousand phones, which is the risk that actually matters here.

/interface list member

/ip firewall nat add chain=srcnat action=masquerade out-interface-list=WAN


  
R1 — one table per job, and rules that pick the table
! R1 — C8300: policy routing with tracked next hops instead of routing tables.
! IOS PBR needs fixed next hops, so each Starlink keeps its own router (not bypass mode)
! and hands R1 a stable LAN address: A = 192.168.1.1, B = 192.168.2.1, 5G = 192.168.8.1.
route-map PBR-ZONES permit 10
 match ip address CRIT-SRC                     ! staff, scanners, tills
 set ip next-hop verify-availability 192.168.1.1 1 track 1
 set ip next-hop verify-availability 192.168.8.1 2 track 3
route-map PBR-ZONES permit 20
 match ip address PROD-SRC                     ! the stream
 set ip next-hop verify-availability 192.168.2.1 1 track 2
 set ip next-hop verify-availability 192.168.1.1 2 track 1
route-map PBR-ZONES permit 30
 match ip address PUBLIC-SRC                   ! public and press
 set ip next-hop verify-availability 192.168.1.1 1 track 1
 set interface Null0                           ! A down: no other line for them
interface GigabitEthernet0/0/2.900
 ip policy route-map PBR-ZONES
/routing table
add name=crit fib
add name=prod fib
/ip route
# main (public, press): Starlink A only
add dst-address=0.0.0.0/0 gateway=1.1.1.1 target-scope=11 check-gateway=ping distance=1
# crit (staff, scanners, tills): Starlink A, then 5G
add dst-address=0.0.0.0/0 gateway=1.1.1.1 target-scope=11 check-gateway=ping distance=1 routing-table=crit
add dst-address=0.0.0.0/0 gateway=100.64.0.1 distance=2 routing-table=crit comment="crit via 5G"
# prod (live stream): Starlink B, then Starlink A
add dst-address=0.0.0.0/0 gateway=9.9.9.9 target-scope=11 check-gateway=ping distance=1 routing-table=prod
add dst-address=0.0.0.0/0 gateway=1.1.1.1 target-scope=11 check-gateway=ping distance=2 routing-table=prod
/routing rule
add dst-address=10.80.0.0/16 action=lookup-only-in-table table=main comment="site stays on site"
add src-address=10.80.10.0/24 action=lookup table=crit
add src-address=10.80.20.0/24 action=lookup table=crit
add src-address=10.80.25.0/24 action=lookup table=crit
add src-address=10.80.30.0/24 action=lookup table=prod


  
Read it by zone. The public and press use the main table: Starlink A, and nothing else. If A fails they lose internet until it returns. That's the deliberate price of keeping B for the stream and 5G for the gates and tills: no route in that table points at either. The critical zones fall back from Starlink A to 5G and never compete with the stream. The stream has Starlink B to itself and borrows Starlink A only if B dies. The first rule is the lesson from the gaming center: without it, traffic between zones would be looked up in tables that only know default routes, and leave the site.


  

    Checkpoint: on the bench, then in the field
    
  • /routing rule print and /ip route print where routing-table=crit: as above, with the probe gateways filled in by the DHCP scripts.
  • Stow Starlink A from its app: scanners and tills continue via 5G within ~20 s; the public and press lose internet (shown on a test phone); the stream keeps running on B.
  • Stow Starlink B instead: the stream moves to A; nothing else notices.

  police cir 60000000
interface GigabitEthernet0/0/2.50
 service-policy output PUBLIC-CAP
/queue type
add name=pcq-pub-down kind=pcq pcq-classifier=dst-address pcq-rate=2M
add name=pcq-pub-up kind=pcq pcq-classifier=src-address pcq-rate=1M
/queue simple add name=public target=10.80.48.0/21 max-limit=8M/60M queue=pcq-pub-up/pcq-pub-down


  
2 Mb/s is enough for messaging, maps and social media, which is what a festival crowd wants; it's not enough to stream video, which is the point. The 60/8 Mb/s cap leaves most of Starlink A for the critical zones, which are tiny in volume but need their packets to go through immediately. With the critical zones unshaped and the public capped well below the line rate, the queue never forms where it would hurt a payment.


  

    Checkpoint: on the bench, then in the field
    
  • Saturday evening, food court full: /queue simple print stats where name=public shows the cap working, and a test card payment completes in its usual time.
  • A single phone's speed test tops out at about 2 Mb/s.

Residual Risk

  
What a temporary network can't fully control, and what covers each gap.


  
    
    
  • Lookalike public SSIDs. Anyone can broadcast "FESTIVAL-Free" from a phone. Signage with the real network name helps a little; the real protection is that nothing sensitive ever uses the public network.
    • 
    
  • Vendor terminals you've never seen before. The network can't vouch for twenty unknown devices. It keeps each one away from everything except the internet, and the payment terminals' own certification covers the rest.
    • 
    
  • Physical access in an open field. Zone boxes sit at the foot of masts in public areas. Lockable enclosures, cable guards, and unused switch ports disabled in the template.
    • 
    
  • Weather and heat. Equipment in sealed boxes in July sun overheats. Ventilated, shaded enclosures and a midday temperature check are part of the daily routine.
    • 
    
  • Crew credentials. Staff tablets and the NOC laptop hold keys to everything. They stay with named crew members and are wiped at teardown like the rest.
    • 
  


  
  
17

  
The Attacker's View

  
I test event networks like this for a living. At a festival, availability is the security property — here's what I'd try, and what absorbs it.


  
    
    
  • Knock the gates and bars offline. A deauth storm or a cut uplink is the attack that actually hurts — "the gates and bars can't stop." Scanners and tills ride separate VLANs and queues, scans cache locally, and 4G/Starlink failover covers a dead WAN, so a flood on the public SSID never reaches the paid work.
    • 
    
  • Hunt the card tills. Twenty vendor terminals are devices I've never seen, and one may ship backdoored. Each is pinned to the internet and its payment processor only — isolated from the scanners, the NOC and every other vendor — so a bad till has nowhere to pivot.
    • 
    
  • Stand up "FESTIVAL-Free". A lookalike SSID from a backpack harvests attendees, but it yields nothing of the organiser's: nothing sensitive ever touches the public network, so signage is the only thing it dents.
    • 
    
  • Plug into a zone box at a mast. The field boxes are physically reachable, so unused switch ports are disabled in the template and management lives on its own VLAN; a patched-in laptop lands in a dead port.
    • 
    
  • Lean on the press tent. Journalists' laptops are untrusted by definition; the press VLAN forwards to the internet only, nowhere near production or ticketing.
    •

    What actually changes in the design
    
Policy routing wants fixed next hops. MikroTik routing tables work happily with gateways learned by DHCP and updated by a script; IOS PBR needs addresses it can track. So on Cisco each Starlink keeps its own router, adding a double-NAT that nothing on this network minds. Per-phone limits move to the Wi-Fi controller, which is the more natural place anyway. Everything else translates directly.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Build it twice. Assemble and test the whole network on a bench; in the field, only mount and plug in. Generated configs mean nobody types one at 07:00.
    2. 
      
  2. Star backhaul from the NOC. One link per zone means one failure costs one zone, and the NOC can see which.
    3. 
      
  3. Put Wi-Fi where people stay put. A small, dense public network in the food court beats thin coverage of a whole field.
    4. 
      
  4. Size the crowd's network on purpose. A /21, 15-minute leases, a bigger DNS cache, and a hard per-phone limit.
    5. 
      
  5. Let routing tables assign the lines. Critical zones fall back to 5G, the stream owns its Starlink, and the public has no route to anything else, so there's nothing to filter.
    6. 
      
  6. Design for the failures you can't prevent. Offline-capable scanners make a dropped gate link a non-event.
    7. 
      
  7. Leave nothing behind. Export the lessons, then reset every node to its staging config before it goes back in the case.
