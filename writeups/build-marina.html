This is part 11 of the Build series, and the one that fights the environment hardest. A marina is a small ISP sitting on a pier: the paying customers are strangers on boats out over the water, the plant runs down floating pontoons that move and corrode, and the two things that must never stop — card payments at the fuel dock and the shore-power that keeps bilge pumps running — share a site with a public hotspot for eighty boats.

    
The design leans on three ideas: carry everything to the pontoons on fibre, not copper, so the sea can't bridge a fault or a surge back into the office; treat every boat as a stranger and bill it like one; and keep the money and the operational systems on their own islands that the guest Wi-Fi can't touch even when it's full.

The Brief

  
A fictional but very typical Cretan marina: a sheltered harbour, eighty berths, open all year, busiest from Easter to October.


  
Four floating pontoons (A–D) hold eighty berths, from small day-boats to a few forty-metre yachts. A stone quay runs along the shore with the capitaneria office (booking system, VoIP, staff PCs), a chandlery and café with a till, and a fuel dock at the end of the breakwater with its own card terminal. Each berth has a shore-power pedestal with a metered socket and water. Liveaboards stay for months and work from their boats; transients arrive in the evening and want Wi-Fi and a shower. Cameras watch the pontoon heads, the fuel dock and the gate. Internet is fibre to the office, with 5G as backup. The installer manages it from Heraklion.

02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Each one is where a marina stops behaving like the other networks in this series.


  
1 · Every boat is a stranger

  
The villa treats one booking as one trust group and lets devices see each other. A marina is the opposite: the boat in A17 and the boat in A18 are two unrelated customers who happen to share an antenna. So the berth network has client isolation on, and access is sold per berth as a voucher, not handed out as one shared key that leaks down the dock.


  
2 · Fibre, not copper, down the pontoons

  
Copper between the shore and a floating pontoon is a bad idea three ways: the run is long, the two ends sit at different ground potentials over salt water (galvanic current), and a mast is a lightning rod wired straight back to your office switch. Single-mode fibre solves all three — it carries hundreds of metres, it's a dielectric so there's no galvanic path and no surge conductor, and PoE for the antenna is generated locally in the pontoon box.


  
3 · 2.4 GHz sectors over the water

  
Over open water with masts in the way, reach beats throughput. 2.4 GHz travels further and diffracts around rigging better than 5 GHz, and a boat only needs enough for streaming and video calls, not a gigabit. Sector antennas on each pontoon head aim down the fairway so the signal follows the line of boats instead of spraying across the harbour.


  
4 · Billing on the box, not in the cloud

  
A cloud captive-portal is one more thing that breaks when the WAN does — and it puts the customer list on someone else's server. RouterOS runs the Hotspot and User Manager on the router itself: vouchers, quotas and per-berth accounting live on hardware the marina owns, and a boat that's already logged in keeps working through a WAN outage.

04

  
The Policy Matrix

  
Rows start, columns receive. Default deny. Payment reaches one destination; the berths reach only the sea of the internet.


  

    
  


  
Two cells carry the design. Payment → Internet is limited to the acquirer's hosts on 443 and nothing else, so a compromised till can't be a pivot. Berths → Berths is denied: client isolation means the boat next door is as unreachable as a boat in another harbour, which is the opposite of the villa's rule and the whole point of selling access per berth.

The build

  
Bottom to Top, One Layer at a Time

  
Rack and office first, water last, billing and backup at the end. Every step ends with a checkpoint.

  
Ten steps: the core switch, the router, fibre to the pontoons, Wi-Fi over the water, the hotspot and billing, the firewall, dual-WAN, fair-sharing, remote care, and power with hardening. This is the step that keeps the sea out of the office. Each pontoon head is a sealed outdoor box with an SFP fibre uplink and a small PoE switch; the fibre is a dielectric, so there is no copper bridging two different ground potentials over salt water and no metallic path for a mast strike to follow back to the rack. The box powers the sector antenna locally and picks up the pontoon's shore-power meters and camera. Treat it as a consumable: when salt or lightning eventually takes one, a replacement restores its config from the hub and is back in minutes.

  
Fibre over water is not fibre in a building
Use outdoor, gel-filled or armoured single-mode with marine-rated conduit along the pontoon, and leave a service loop at the hinge where the pontoon moves against the shore — that flex point is where a cable dies first. Terminate with pre-polished connectors in the dry box, not in the open air over the water.

  
Checkpoint: test this before building the next step

  • From the office, the pontoon box answers on 10.80.99.21; the shore-power meters and the camera get their reserved addresses.

One controller on R1 provisions every pontoon AP with the same two SSIDs: an open Marina-WiFi that the hotspot will gate in step 5, and a hidden WPA3 Marina-Staff for the harbour-master's tablet. The berth SSID runs on 2.4 GHz on purpose — over open water, with masts and rigging in the way, reach and diffraction matter more than the throughput 5 GHz would add. The sectors point down the fairway so the beam follows the line of boats.

  
The mast is the obstacle
A metal mast and a boom full of rigging sit right between the antenna and the cockpit where people use their phones. Mount the sector high on the pontoon-head pole so it looks over the near boats, accept that 2.4 GHz is congested (every boat's own hotspot is on it), and plan co-channel reuse across pontoons the way you would cells, not like one big AP.

The open SSID becomes a product: a voucher per berth, a rate cap, a fair quota, all issued and accounted on the router.

/user-manager set enabled=yes
/user-manager/router add name=self address=127.0.0.1 shared-secret="********"
# a rate cap and a fair daily quota per boat; a week-long profile for a transient berth
/user-manager/limitation add name=lim-berth rate-limit=20M/5M
/user-manager/profile add name=berth-week name-for-users="7-day berth" validity=7d
/user-manager/profile-limitation add profile=berth-week limitation=lim-berth
# one voucher per slip, printed at the office on arrival; the code is never logged
/user-manager/user add name=A17 password="<voucher>" shared-users=3
/user-manager/user add name=A17 attributes=... # bind to profile berth-week
# walled garden: the booking page and the card host stay reachable before login
/ip hotspot walled-garden
add dst-host=book.marina.example action=allow
add dst-host=*.acquirer.example action=allow

  
The hotspot turns the open Wi-Fi into something the office sells. User Manager — RouterOS's own RADIUS server — runs on R1 itself, so the customer list and the accounting never leave the marina's hardware, and a boat that has already logged in keeps working even if the WAN drops. The office prints a voucher per berth on arrival, tied to a profile that sets the stay length, the rate cap and a fair daily quota. The walled garden keeps the booking page and the card host reachable before anyone logs in, so a guest can pay for access and the fuel dock can take a card without a session.

  
The voucher is loot — treat it like it
Generate voucher codes with real randomness, hand them out on paper, and never write the code to the log or an e-mail body (log that a voucher was issued, not the code). Set shared-users to match a boat's realistic device count; one voucher for a whole pontoon is how a marina ends up running an open relay.

  
Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
  • A phone on Marina-WiFi is redirected to the portal; a valid voucher logs in and the rate cap applies.
  • /ip hotspot active print and /user-manager/session print show the session and its accounting.
  • Before login, the booking and acquirer hosts load; nothing else does.

The forward chain is the marina's property line. The payment VLAN is allowed exactly one thing — the acquirer on 443 — so a compromised terminal is a dead end, not a pivot onto the booking server or the hotspot. The operational VLAN (shore-power, fuel controller, AIS) answers only to staff on V10 and otherwise reaches just its vendors' clouds. The berths reach the internet and, by the absence of any rule and by client isolation, nothing and no one else on the network.

  
Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
  • From the till VLAN, only the acquirer answers on 443; the booking server and the internet at large do not.
  • From Marina-WiFi, a scan of 10.80.0.0/16 finds only the gateway; no other boat, no camera, no meter.
  • From a boat, the shore-power controller and the fuel dock don't answer; from a staff PC they do.

Dual-WAN: Fibre & 5G

  
Fibre carries the harbour; 5G catches it when the fibre drops, with the hotspot throttled so staff and payments keep their share.

  
▲ Show step 7 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config

  
R1 — fibre primary, 5G backup, failover by recursive route
! R1 — fibre on Gi0/0/0 (DHCP), 5G on the cellular interface as the backup
interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0
 description fibre
 ip address dhcp
 ip nat outside
ip sla 1
 icmp-echo 1.1.1.1
 frequency 5
ip sla schedule 1 life forever start-time now
track 1 ip sla 1 reachability
ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 dhcp 1 track 1
ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 Cellular0/2/0 2
ip access-list standard NAT-ALL
 permit 10.80.0.0 0.0.255.255
ip nat inside source list NAT-ALL interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0 overload
! police the berth VLAN down hard when it rides the cellular backup
/interface list member add list=WAN interface=ether1
/interface list member add list=WAN interface=ether2
/ip dhcp-client add interface=ether1 add-default-route=no use-peer-dns=no comment="fibre"
# 5G as a passthrough modem; its default route sits at a worse distance (backup)
/ip dhcp-client add interface=ether2 default-route-distance=2 use-peer-dns=no comment="5G"
/ip route
# recursive main route: reachable only while the probe through fibre answers
add dst-address=1.1.1.1/32 gateway=ether1 scope=10 comment="probe via fibre"
add dst-address=0.0.0.0/0 gateway=1.1.1.1 target-scope=11 check-gateway=ping distance=1 \
    comment="main: fibre"
/ip firewall nat add chain=srcnat action=masquerade out-interface-list=WAN
# on 5G, the hotspot (berth Wi-Fi) is throttled hard; staff, payments and VoIP are not
/ip firewall mangle add chain=forward action=mark-packet new-packet-mark=berth-5g \
    in-interface=v20-berth out-interface=ether2 passthrough=no
/queue tree add name=berth-on-5g parent=global packet-mark=berth-5g max-limit=15M \
    queue=pcq-download-default

  
Fibre is the primary path with a recursive route whose health is a ping through the fibre gateway; 5G sits behind it at a worse distance and takes over on failure. The difference from the villa is who gets protected: here the guests are the ones squeezed on the backup. When the harbour runs on 5G, the berth hotspot is throttled to a trickle so that a card authorisation, a VoIP call to a chandler, and the booking system keep working. Paying boats stay connected, just slowly, which is the honest trade on a backup link.

  
Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
  • Pull the fibre: within ~20 s the harbour is on 5G; a card payment and a VoIP call still complete.
  • During the outage, a speed test on Marina-WiFi shows the berth throttle; staff traffic is not throttled.
  • Fibre back: the main route returns within a minute.

CAKE shares the fibre fairly between boats; VoIP and card authorisations ride above the crowd.

# CAKE shares the fibre fairly per host first: one busy boat cannot starve the others
/queue type
add name=cake-up kind=cake cake-diffserv=diffserv4 cake-nat=yes cake-flowmode=dual-srchost
add name=cake-down kind=cake cake-diffserv=diffserv4 cake-flowmode=dual-dsthost
/ip firewall mangle
# lift VoIP and card-auth into the priority tin so a full hotspot never delays them
add chain=prerouting action=change-dscp new-dscp=46 src-address=10.80.10.0/24 \
    protocol=udp dst-port=10000-20000 comment="VoIP RTP"
add chain=prerouting action=change-dscp new-dscp=34 src-address=10.80.50.0/24 \
    comment="card auth"
add chain=forward action=mark-packet new-packet-mark=from-fibre in-interface=ether1 \
    passthrough=no
/queue tree
add name=wan-up parent=ether1 queue=cake-up max-limit=90M
add name=wan-down parent=global packet-mark=from-fibre queue=cake-down max-limit=450M

  
CAKE gives per-host fairness, so one liveaboard pulling a system update can't starve forty other boats, and its NAT mode sees the real inside host behind the masquerade. On top of that, the two flows that must never wait — VoIP from the office and card authorisations from the payment VLAN — are marked into CAKE's priority tin, so even a saturated hotspot doesn't add a beat of delay to a phone call or a card tap. Set the limit just under the fibre's real throughput, because CAKE only controls latency when the queue lives in R1.

  
Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
  • One boat runs a big download while another holds a video call: the call stays clear.
  • A card authorisation during a saturated hotspot completes in the same time as on an idle link.
  • /queue tree print stats: drops on wan-down under load; the priority tin stays near-empty.

Residual Risk

  
What the network can't do on its own, and what covers each gap.


  
    
    
  • The berth SSID is open. Authentication is the hotspot, not WPA, so traffic over the air is unencrypted until the user's own app-layer TLS takes over. That's normal for paid public Wi-Fi, but the welcome card should tell guests to treat it like any café network and lean on a VPN for anything sensitive.
    • 
    
  • 2.4 GHz is a shared, crowded band. Every boat's own hotspot, every Bluetooth speaker and the marina's own APs fight for it. Co-channel planning helps; a bad-neighbour boat with a rogue AP on the same channel is a support call, not a config fix.
    • 
    
  • Payment terminals still trust their vendor. The network fences them to the acquirer; the terminals' firmware and the vendor's P2PE are what actually protect card data. Keep them in PCI scope and let the acquirer's kit do the cryptography.
    • 
    
  • Shore-power and the fuel controller are safety systems. The network keeps boats away from them, but a staff PC that gets malware is inside V10 and can reach them. Staff endpoint hygiene and MFA on the management tools matter as much as the VLAN.
    • 
    
  • One operator, many harbours. The hub's keys open every marina it manages. A per-site WireGuard key and the hub's own MFA are what make a single compromise survivable.
    •

The Attacker's View

  
I test networks like this for a living. Here's what I'd try against a marina full of strangers on boats, and why each one is a decision rather than an oversight.


  
    
    
  • Sit on the open berth Wi-Fi and sweep. The obvious move on an open SSID is to scan the neighbours and the infrastructure. Client isolation on V20 and the default-deny forward chain mean a boat sees only the gateway; the meters, cameras, tills and other boats are on VLANs no berth rule reaches.
    • 
    
  • Evil-twin Marina-WiFi. A rogue AP from a boat harvests portal credentials and MITMs guests. It buys nothing of the marina's: captured vouchers still only unlock VLAN 20 to the internet, and the payment, OT and staff VLANs are unreachable from there whatever credential you hold.
    • 
    
  • Go after the fuel-dock card terminal. If I can reach the till VLAN I'd pivot from it — except V50 is allowed exactly one destination, the acquirer on 443. No lateral path to the booking server, no general egress, nothing to pivot to.
    • 
    
  • Reach the shore-power or fuel controller. These are the systems with physical consequences. They live on V30 with a single inbound door from staff V10; from a boat they don't answer, and they can't be reached from the payment or camera VLANs either.
    • 
    
  • Skip the harbour and phish the operator. The WireGuard hub holds a key to every marina it manages, so the efficient attack is the operator's laptop, not the dish on the breakwater. The per-site key and the hub's MFA are what make that single compromise survivable — not anything in the marina's config.
    •

    What actually changes in the design
    
The billing is the big divergence. RouterOS runs the hotspot, the RADIUS server and the voucher store on the router you already bought; the Cisco equivalent is ISE (or a third-party captive-portal appliance) as a separate box with its own licence, and the accounting lives there instead of on the marina's router. And there is no CAKE, so per-boat fairness becomes a shaper with fair-queue plus an LLQ class for VoIP and card auth.
For a single marina, MikroTik is the clear fit. Cisco makes sense only when a port authority already standardises on it across many harbours.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Every boat is a stranger. Client-isolate the berth network and sell access per berth, the mirror image of the villa's one-group rule.
    2. 
      
  2. Carry the pontoons on fibre, not copper. It beats the distance, the galvanic path and the lightning conductor in one move, and generates PoE locally.
    3. 
      
  3. Pick reach over throughput over water. 2.4 GHz sectors down the fairway, planned like cells, beat a faster band that can't get past a mast.
    4. 
      
  4. Own the billing. Hotspot and User Manager on the router keep the customer list and the money-path on the marina's own hardware and survive a WAN outage.
    5. 
      
  5. Seal the money and the safety systems. Payment reaches only the acquirer; shore-power and fuel answer only to staff; neither shares fate with a full hotspot.
    6. 
      
  6. Build the pontoon boxes to be replaced. Salt and lightning win eventually; a spare that restores from the hub turns an outage into a short swap.
