Build: The
Esports Arena Network

A two-day regional final: eight teams, a stage with two five-seat booths, 1,500 spectators, casters, observers, replay, and a live broadcast watched by tens of thousands. The players need the fairest, quietest network in the building; the production needs the busiest; and the two must never meet. We build it on Cisco from the stage outwards in 11 tested steps.

This is part 8 of the Build series, and the big brother of the gaming center. There, the threat was customers attacking each other and the goal was low latency for everyone. Here, ten people on a stage are playing for prize money in front of an audience, and the network has three new jobs: give both teams exactly the same path to the match server, make sure no player can see the broadcast (which would reveal the other team's positions), and keep enough evidence to settle a dispute about lag.

    
At the same time, a production team is moving a dozen high-bitrate video feeds around the building and sending the finished show to the world twice over. The design keeps those two worlds on separate hardware, connected only where the rules say.

01

  
The Brief

  
A fictional regional final in a 1,500-seat arena. The tournament operator owns the rules; the network has to enforce some of them.


  
Two teams of five play on stage at a time, in booths on either side of a central stage rack that holds two match servers (main and hot spare) and the referee's console. Six more teams warm up in a bootcamp room with 40 PCs and practice servers. The production gallery has four observer PCs (in-game camera operators), replay and graphics machines, the casters' feeds, and two encoders. A press room has wired desks. The arena has public Wi-Fi for spectators. The tournament runs on the publisher's platform, which needs the players' PCs to log in and run the publisher's anti-cheat.


  

    
RequirementWhere it comes from, and what it forces

    
      
R1 Fair pathsBoth teams must have the same latency to the match server. Same switch, same port type, same cable length, one hop.

      
R2 No ghostingA player watching the live broadcast would see the opponents' positions. Players get no route to any stream or video site, only an allowlist.

      
R3 Delayed spectatingObservers watch the match through the server's spectator port, which runs on a delay. Production reaches that port and nothing else on the stage.

      
R4 Evidence for disputes"My game lagged" claims are settled with data. All stage traffic is recorded for the length of the event.

      
R5 Broadcast never dropsThe show leaves the building twice, from two encoders over two ISPs.

      
R6 Production bandwidthTwelve NDI video feeds at ~125–250 Mb/s each move around the gallery. 10G everywhere in production, and multicast under control.

      
R7 Spectators stay out1,500 phones on public Wi-Fi with no path to anything that matters, and a hard per-device limit.

      
R8 Change freezeNothing is changed on match days except by the network lead, with the tournament director's approval.

    
  


  
  
02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Each one exists because an esports final is judged on fairness first and speed second.


  
1 · The stage pod routes itself

  
Players and match servers live on the same switch, a C9300-24UX in the stage rack, but in different VLANs, so an ACL can sit between them. If that switch only did Layer 2, every packet between a player and the server would go to the core and back. So STAGE-SW routes its own pod: player-to-server traffic is one switch, one routing lookup, for both teams. Its single routed uplink to the core is the pod's only door, and that door has its own ACL.


  
2 · An allowlist enforced by DNS

  
Players need the publisher's login, matchmaking and anti-cheat services, which run on large cloud and CDN address ranges that change constantly. An IP allowlist would break mid-match. Instead, players use a tournament resolver that only answers the domain names in the publisher's tournament guide; every other name gets "does not exist". The switch ACL blocks any other DNS and anything that isn't HTTPS. The locked player image (no admin rights, encrypted DNS disabled) closes what the network can't.


  
3 · Capture everything on stage

  
A SPAN session on STAGE-SW copies every packet of every player port and both servers to a capture box with a rolling 48-hour buffer. That's a few hundred GB for a two-day event, a cheap disk, and the end of most lag disputes, because the referee can show the exact packet loss and latency for the seat and minute in question. Players agree to this in the tournament rules.


  
4 · Redundancy in the application, not just the network

  
The broadcast doesn't rely on WAN failover. Two encoders send the same program over two ISPs to a cloud ingest that picks a healthy feed. A failing ISP costs nothing visible. The network's job is simply to keep each encoder on its own line, which is what policy routing does.

    "Same path" means more than same switch
    
Both teams on one switch is the obvious part. Less obvious: identical cable lengths from the same batch; both teams' ports on the same forwarding ASIC (true for every port on a 24-port C9300; on larger switches, check which port groups share an ASIC); identical negotiated speed (check show interfaces status after the PCs are in, as one PC with an older NIC at 1G against nine at 2.5G is a fairness complaint waiting to happen); and Energy-Efficient Ethernet off, because its wake-up delay is tiny but not identical between NICs. Write all of it into the tournament's technical rules, so it was checked rather than assumed.

  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • show interfaces status: all ten player ports at the same speed and duplex.
  • A player PC pings the match server; the round-trip time from a team A seat and a team B seat is the same to within a few microseconds (measure with a high-resolution tool such as sockperf, not Windows ping).
  • show port-security: one sticky MAC per player port.

/interface bridge vlan add bridge=bridge vlan-ids=30,99 tagged=bond-core

The production team uses NDI to move video between machines: each observer's game view, the casters' cameras, replay outputs and graphics all become network sources. A full-bandwidth 1080p60 NDI stream is about 125–150 Mb/s, and a vision mixer may pull a dozen at once, which is why the gallery is 10G and the uplink is 2×10G. Two settings keep it stable:

  
    
    
  • Discovery through an NDI Discovery Server (10.90.30.5) instead of mDNS. Every NDI machine is pointed at it, so sources show up reliably and discovery traffic never needs to cross a router.
    • 
    
  • IGMP snooping with a querier. NDI is unicast by default; if the mixer turns on multicast for a source, snooping makes sure that stream only reaches the ports that joined it. Without a querier in a VLAN with no multicast router, snooping tables age out and multicast floods every port.
    • 
  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • The mixer sees all NDI sources via the Discovery Server; twelve full-bandwidth sources play without drops.
  • Enable multicast on one source: show ip igmp snooping groups lists only the ports that joined it.

The ports are an example: 27015 for the game and 27020 for the delayed spectator feed are the defaults for Source-engine titles. Every publisher's tournament guide lists its own, and each one becomes a named, commented line. The ops VLAN (referee, admin consoles) has no inbound ACL; it can reach players and servers for administration, and the players' ACL only lets them answer it.


  

    Why "HTTPS to anywhere" is still an allowlist
    
Line 80 lets players open HTTPS to any address, which looks like the opposite of an allowlist. But a player PC only learns addresses through DNS-ALLOW, which refuses every name except the publisher's, and line 50 blocks every other resolver inside the building. Two gaps remain, and both are closed on the PC, not the network: encrypted DNS (DoH) to a public resolver, and connecting to an IP address typed by hand. The tournament image disables DoH by policy, players have no admin rights, and the referee's checklist includes a quick look at each seat's browser before every match.

The capture box has two network ports: cap0 on Te1/0/24 receives the SPAN copy (a SPAN destination port doesn't carry normal traffic), and a second port on Te1/0/14 in the ops VLAN is how the referee reaches it. A full match generates a few GB of game traffic; 48 hours of 10-minute files fits comfortably on a 2 TB disk. The files are deleted after the event's dispute window closes, and that retention is stated in the tournament rules players sign.

12 · Step 8 of 11

Putting each ISP in its own zone gives the "who may use the backup" rule for free, the same idea as the supermarket's tunnel-only LTE zone. A self-zone policy for the probes isn't needed here, because nothing on the WAN needs to reach the edge itself. If you add one later, remember the echo-reply line from the law firm. The route-map NAT per interface is the law firm's dual-WAN NAT lesson again.

The Broadcast, Sent Twice

  
Two encoders, two ISPs, one cloud ingest that always has a healthy copy.


  

    
  


  
Both encoders take the same program output from the vision mixer and send it with SRT (a protocol built for live video over the public internet: it retransmits lost packets within a fixed latency budget) to two inputs of a cloud production service. Encoder 1 leaves via ISP B because of the policy route; encoder 2 leaves via ISP A by normal routing. The cloud service watches both and forwards one to the streaming platforms. If an ISP, an encoder or a cable fails, it switches within a second. The production network's ACL on the core allows the encoders out to the cloud ingest and nothing else.


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • Both SRT inputs show green in the cloud service, with packet loss and round-trip time per input.
  • Unplug ISP B: the cloud switches to encoder 2's feed and the public stream doesn't visibly blink.

    Egress QoS sees translated addresses
    
By the time a packet reaches the output policy on Gi0/0/0, NAT has already rewritten its source to the ISP A address, so an ACL matching 10.90.10.0/24 there would never match anything. The fix is to classify where the inside addresses are still visible, on the LAN subinterface's input policy, and carry the decision across the router as a qos-group (a label that exists only inside this router). The output policy then queues by qos-group. The 1 Mb/s per spectator is enforced at the APs; this policy makes sure that even 1,500 of them together can't squeeze the critical class below 60% of the line.

  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step

Match-Day Operations

  
A change freeze you can roll back, monitoring the director can read, and nothing improvised.


  
All devices — a known-good config, change logging, one-command rollback
archive

  logging enable
  notify syslog contenttype plaintext
  hidekeys
! the night before each match day, after the scrim passes:
copy running-config flash:matchday-baseline.cfg
! if a change on match day goes wrong:
configure replace flash:matchday-baseline.cfg list
# every device — baseline the night before, roll back by restoring it
/system backup save name=matchday-baseline dont-encrypt=no password=********
/export file=matchday-baseline
# risky change on match day: do it in Safe Mode (Ctrl+X), which undoes itself if you lose the session
# full rollback: /system backup load name=matchday-baseline.backup (reboots the device)


  
The tournament director approves any change on match days, the network lead makes it, and every command is logged with the user who typed it. configure replace puts a device back to the baseline in seconds, without a reload. In the NOC corner of the production gallery, one screen shows the IP SLA probes for both ISPs, the SRT health of both encoders, STAGE-SW's port counters and errors, and the AP client counts. If any of those changes during a match, the network lead sees it first. Management access is limited to VLAN 99 and the ops VLAN (see hardening).


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • Make a harmless change on FOH-SW, then run configure replace: the device returns to the baseline, and the syslog shows both the change and the rollback with usernames.

Scrim Day: The Full Rehearsal

  
The day before doors open, two teams play a full match on stage with the whole production running. Every drill happens during that match.


  

    
Drill (requirement)What must happen

    
      
Fair paths (R1)Latency from every seat to the match server measured with a high-resolution tool: all ten within a few microseconds; all ports at the same speed.

      
No ghosting (R2)On a player PC, any stream or video site fails to resolve; DNS to outside resolvers times out.

      
Delayed spectating (R3)Observers see the match on the spectator delay; nothing else from production reaches the pod.

      
Dispute drill (R4)The referee extracts and graphs one seat's traffic for a chosen two-minute window.

      
Broadcast resilience (R5)Unplug ISP B, then ISP A (one at a time): the cloud output never blinks.

      
Production load (R6)All NDI sources live at full bandwidth for the whole match, no dropped frames at the mixer.

      
Crowd (R7)The public VLAN loaded to its limit: player login and the match unaffected.

Residual Risk

  
What the network can't rule out, and what covers it.


  
    
    
  • The arena's big screens. The network keeps the broadcast off player PCs, but the bowl's screens show the live show. Booth soundproofing, screens that players can't see, and noise-cancelling headsets are the tournament's controls for that.
    • 
    
  • Player devices. Phones are collected before matches; peripherals are wired and inspected. A USB device can't reach the network through the locked image's policies, but checking for one is the referee's job.
    • 
    
  • The publisher's services. If the publisher's login or anti-cheat has an outage, no local design helps. The tournament rules need a pause-and-resume procedure for that case.
    • 
    
  • The capture data. It contains every stage packet. Access is limited to the referee team, and the files are deleted when the dispute window closes.
    • 
    
  • Admin access during the event. Anyone with an admin console can reach the players. Admin accounts are named, logged, and few.

The Attacker's View

  
I test competition networks like this for a living. Here the network enforces tournament rules, not only security — here's what I'd try.


  
    
    
  • Stream-snipe from a player booth. The match is lost the moment a player sees the broadcast or the observers' camera feed. The player-stage VLAN has no path to production, replay or the caster feeds — the segmentation is the anti-cheat rule, enforced in the switch rather than promised.
    • 
    
  • Compromise a player PC through the publisher platform. The locked image logs into the publisher and runs anti-cheat; that VLAN reaches the match servers and the publisher login only, not ops or production, so a popped PC can't touch the show.
    • 
    
  • Stage from the bootcamp room. Forty warm-up PCs are the soft target. Bootcamp is isolated from the live match servers and the stage rack, so practice can never reach the competition.
    • 
    
  • Exfiltrate the referee capture. Every stage packet is recorded — gold for a team filing a dispute. The capture lives on the referee VLAN with no forward path from production or players; taking it means owning the referee console, which is named, logged and few.
    • 
    
  • Jump from the spectator Wi-Fi. Public bowl Wi-Fi forwards to the internet only; from a seat I reach the gateway, not the stage, the servers or production.
    •

    What actually changes in the design
    
The stage pod is the hard part. A Catalyst routes the pod in its switching hardware with identical, tiny latency for every port. On MikroTik the pod is routed by a small CCR in software. That's still well under a tenth of a millisecond and the same for both teams, but it's exactly the kind of claim the fairness rules say must be measured, not assumed. Rollback is coarser: there's no configure replace, so match-day changes rely on Safe Mode and a full backup restore.
For a one-off community tournament, MikroTik is a sensible choice. For a broadcast final with prize money, the hardware-routed pod and atomic rollback are worth the Cisco price.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Fairness is a network requirement. Same switch, same ASIC, same cable, same speed, one hop, with EEE off. Measure it and write it into the rules.
    2. 
      
  2. Let the stage pod route itself, so player-to-server traffic never leaves one box and the pod has exactly one door with its own ACL.
    3. 
      
  3. Enforce the player allowlist with DNS. A resolver that answers only the publisher's names, every other resolver blocked, and the locked image closing the last gaps.
    4. 
      
  4. Observers get the delayed feed, never the players. One UDP port is the only way into the pod from production.
    5. 
      
  5. Capture everything on stage. SPAN to a ring buffer turns lag disputes into measurements.
    6. 
      
  6. Make the broadcast redundant at the application level. Two encoders over two ISPs, with policy routing keeping each on its own line.
    7. 
      
  7. Classify before NAT. Mark on the inside interface, queue on the outside.
