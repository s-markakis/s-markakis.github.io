Build: The
Ferry Port Network

A regional ferry port on Crete's north coast: an administration building, a passenger terminal, a cargo shed with customs, a control tower running the harbour's radar, and a fuel yard by the gate. Five buildings along a kilometre of quay, where an excavator or a crane can cut a fibre on any working day. We connect them with a routed ring running authenticated OSPF, give the port two ways to the internet that fail over by themselves, and fence the security systems off, in 11 tested steps.

This is part 13 of the Build series, and the first one where dynamic routing is the point of the design. Every earlier build had one core and one way to each building. A port has five buildings strung along the waterfront, ducts that get dug up, and systems such as the harbour radar that can't wait for someone to re-patch a cable.

    
OSPF is the tool that turns "there is another fibre" into "traffic is already using it". The rest of the post is about making that safe: who is allowed to speak OSPF, how quickly it reacts, how to keep a small outdoor cabinet out of the backbone's business, and how to work on the network while ships are boarding.

The Brief

  
A fictional but typical regional port: two ferry lines, ro-ro freight, about 600,000 passengers a year, almost all of them between May and October.


  
The port authority runs five sites on one property. The administration building holds the offices, the data room and the fibre ISP. The passenger terminal has the ticket desks and the boarding gates. The cargo shed houses customs and the freight office. The control tower at the root of the breakwater runs the vessel traffic service (VTS): radar, AIS and recorded VHF. The fuel and cargo gate yard behind the cargo shed has the gate cameras, the barrier and the fuel dispensers. All of them have CCTV and card-access doors, required by the port facility security plan under the ISPS Code.




    
RequirementWhere it comes from, and what it forces

    
R1 Survive a cut ductWorks on the quay are constant. Losing any one fibre must not stop any building, and traffic must move in under a second, before the ticketing app or a camera stream notices.
R2 Two ways outTicketing and email must keep working when the fibre ISP fails. A Starlink dish on the tower is the backup, and the switch-over is automatic in both directions.
R3 Security systems fenced offISPS Code and NIS2: CCTV, access control, VTS and the fuel PLCs are reachable only from their own servers, and have no internet path.
R4 The tower stands aloneThe VTS keeps working with every fibre to the tower cut. Nothing it needs during a watch may depend on another building.

R6 A small yard, a small footprintThe yard cabinet holds an industrial switch. It should not have to carry or process the port's full routing table.
R7 Work during the seasonFibre splicing, upgrades and reboots happen while ferries are boarding. Maintenance must move traffic away first, not cause the outage.
R8 EvidenceNIS2 incident reporting within 24 hours: routing changes, config changes and security events logged centrally with synchronised time.
R9 Passengers stay outsideThousands of passenger phones a day never touch the authority's network.

  


  
  
02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
All four come from the same fact: the port is long and thin, and things along it get broken.


  
1 · Every building routes for itself

  
One Catalyst 9300 per building is the default gateway for that building's VLANs, and the links between buildings are routed /31s. No VLAN crosses the quay, so there is no spanning tree to wait for and no broadcast domain that spans five buildings. It is also what makes R4 true: the tower's VLANs are routed inside the tower.


  
2 · A ring with a diagonal, in two ducts

  
The four cores form a ring: two sides in the quay duct, two in the access-road duct, plus a diagonal from administration to cargo. Any single fibre can fail with every building still connected, and the diagonal gives the busiest pair (data room and cargo) a direct path. OSPF with BFD finds the new path in under a second.


  
3 · One backbone area, one stub

  
Four cores and two edges are far too few to need multiple backbone areas: area 0 covers them all, and every router sees the same topology. The yard gets area 20, totally stubby, because it is a single outdoor cabinet that only needs a default route. That decision is about the IE-3400's workload and blast radius, not about scale.


  
4 · Defaults that follow health, not configuration

  
Neither edge injects a default unconditionally. Each one originates it only while its own uplink passes a probe, with a lower metric for fibre than for Starlink. Failover and recovery need no human and no script: OSPF already knows which exit works.

The Policy Matrix

  
Rows start, columns receive. Default deny. Routing reaches everything; this is what is allowed to use it.


  


  


  
The interesting column is Security / OT VLANs: nothing may start a connection into it. The NVR, the access-control server and the fuel-management server reach their devices because the devices' ACLs let replies out, not because anything is allowed in. A compromised staff PC has no route through the policy to a camera, a door controller or a fuel PLC, even though OSPF has a route to all of them.

The Ring: Routed Fibre Along the Quay

  
Four core switches, five fibre links, no spanning tree anywhere along the quay.


  
▲ Show step 1 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  
Each building has one Catalyst 9300 that routes for the whole building. The links between them are routed ports with /31 addresses: no VLAN stretches from one building to another, so a loop or a broadcast storm in the cargo shed stays in the cargo shed. The four cores form a ring along two different duct routes (the quay and the port access road), plus one diagonal from administration to cargo through the access road, so every core has at least two ways to every other core.

    Why /31s, and why routed ports instead of a VLAN ring
    
A /31 (RFC 3021) is the natural size for a link with exactly two ends: no network or broadcast address to waste, and the address plan for the whole ring fits in one /28. Both IOS-XE and RouterOS 7 accept it.
The alternative, trunking a "core VLAN" round the ring and letting spanning tree block one side, is how many ports were built. It works until someone plugs a loop into a container office, and then four buildings go down together. Routed ports turn every building into its own failure domain.

  

  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • show ip interface brief | include Te1/1: every ring port up/up with its /31.
  • From CORE-ADMIN, ping 10.255.1.1, .6 and .9: each directly connected neighbour answers.
  • From CORE-ADMIN, ping 10.255.0.3 fails. Nothing knows the loopbacks yet; that is the next step.

  


  
  
06 · Step 2 of 11

  
OSPF Area 0

  
One area for the backbone, point-to-point adjacencies, and silence on every port that has no router behind it.


  
▲ Show step 2 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  
The whole ring and both edges are area 0. With four core routers there is no reason for more areas in the backbone: the link-state database is a few dozen LSAs, and one area means every router computes the same picture of the port. The only other area is the fuel yard in step 7, which exists for a different reason.

  
CORE-ADMIN — OSPF process, area 0 on the ring
router ospf 1
 router-id 10.255.0.1
! 100G reference: 10G = cost 10, 1G = cost 100 (the default 100M makes them all 1)
 auto-cost reference-bandwidth 100000
! nothing talks OSPF unless named below: no hellos into a user VLAN, ever
 passive-interface default
 no passive-interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/1
 no passive-interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/2
 no passive-interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/3
interface Loopback0
 ip ospf 1 area 0
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/1
! two routers on a cable: no DR/BDR election, adjacency comes up faster
 ip ospf network point-to-point
 ip ospf 1 area 0
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/2
 ip ospf network point-to-point
 ip ospf 1 area 0
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/3
 ip ospf network point-to-point
 ip ospf 1 area 0
/routing ospf instance add name=ospf1 version=2 router-id=10.255.0.1
/routing ospf area add name=backbone area-id=0.0.0.0 instance=ospf1
# no reference bandwidth on RouterOS: set the cost the Cisco side computes (10G = 10)
/routing ospf interface-template
add area=backbone interfaces=sfp-sfpplus1,sfp-sfpplus2,sfp-sfpplus3 \
    type=ptp cost=10
add area=backbone networks=10.255.0.1/32 passive

  
Three choices in those few lines matter more than they look:

  
  • Interface-mode OSPF (ip ospf 1 area 0 on each interface) instead of network statements: what runs OSPF is visible on the interface itself, and a new SVI can't join by accident because its address happens to fall inside a wildcard.
  • Network type point-to-point on every ring link: a fibre with two routers on it doesn't need a designated router. The adjacency skips the DR election wait and comes up as soon as the hellos match.
  • passive-interface default: every interface is silent unless explicitly named. The user VLANs are still advertised, but no hello is ever sent into them, so a laptop running an OSPF daemon on the staff network finds nobody to talk to.

  

    The reference bandwidth must be the same on every router
    
IOS defaults to a 100 Mb/s reference, so a 1G link and a 10G link both cost 1 and OSPF can't tell them apart. With auto-cost reference-bandwidth 100000, 10G costs 10 and the 1G links to the edges and the yard cost 100. Set it on one router and forget another, and that router computes different costs and can choose paths the others don't expect. RouterOS has no reference bandwidth at all, so on a mixed network the MikroTik side sets cost= explicitly to the value Cisco computes.

  

  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • show ip ospf neighbor on CORE-ADMIN: three neighbours, all FULL/  - (the dash means point-to-point, no DR).
  • show ip route ospf: the other three loopbacks, each learned directly from its own neighbour (CORE-ADMIN is one 10G hop from every core).
  • From CORE-ADMIN, traceroute 10.255.0.3 goes direct over the diagonal; shut Te1/1/3 and it goes round through TERM or TOWER.

  


  
  
07 · Step 3 of 11

  
Locking the Routing Domain

  
A routing protocol that believes every packet it hears is an open door. Close it before any user port goes live.


  
▲ Show step 3 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  
OSPF trusts its neighbours completely: one forged LSA can pull a building's traffic towards a laptop, or blackhole the default route. A port is exactly where that risk is real: contractors, ship crews and visiting technicians plug into whatever socket is nearest. Passive interfaces already keep OSPF off user VLANs; authentication and TTL security make sure that even on a ring port, only a router with the key that is physically one hop away is believed.

  
CORE-ADMIN — HMAC-SHA-256 key chain on every adjacency
key chain OSPF-KEYS
 key 1
  key-string ********
! RFC 5709: HMAC-SHA-256, not MD5 and never plain text
  cryptographic-algorithm hmac-sha-256
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/1
 ip ospf authentication key-chain OSPF-KEYS
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/2
 ip ospf authentication key-chain OSPF-KEYS
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/3
 ip ospf authentication key-chain OSPF-KEYS
router ospf 1
! drop any OSPF packet that didn't come from a directly connected neighbour
 ttl-security all-interfaces hops 1
# HMAC-SHA-256 on every adjacency-forming template (key id must match the Cisco key 1)
/routing ospf interface-template
set [find where !passive] auth=sha256 auth-id=1 auth-key="********"
# no TTL-security knob: drop OSPF from anything that isn't a ring port instead
/interface list add name=RING
/interface list member
add list=RING interface=sfp-sfpplus1
add list=RING interface=sfp-sfpplus2
add list=RING interface=sfp-sfpplus3
add list=RING interface=sfp-sfpplus4
/ip firewall filter
add chain=input protocol=ospf in-interface-list=!RING action=drop \
    comment="OSPF only on ring ports"

  
ttl-security all-interfaces hops 1 makes the router send OSPF with TTL 255 and accept only packets that still have 255 (or 254 with hops 1), so OSPF packets injected from further away than a directly connected neighbour are dropped before they are processed. RouterOS has no equivalent knob, so the MikroTik pane gets the same effect with an input-chain rule that accepts OSPF only on ring ports.

  

    Plan the key rollover before you need it
    
A key chain can hold two keys with overlapping lifetimes. To rotate, add key 2 on every router with a send-lifetime starting at an agreed time, wait until the moment passes, then remove key 1. Because each router accepts both keys during the overlap, the adjacencies never drop. Changing the single key-string on one router at a time drops each adjacency for as long as the two ends disagree.

  

  

    In Packet Tracer
    
Packet Tracer supports only MD5 authentication (ip ospf message-digest-key 1 md5 with ip ospf authentication message-digest) and has no key chains or TTL security. Use MD5 there to see an adjacency refuse a wrong key; build the SHA-256 version on CML or EVE-NG.

  

  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • show ip ospf interface Te1/1/1: Cryptographic authentication enabled, key id 1, algorithm HMAC-SHA-256.
  • Change the key-string on one side of one link: that adjacency drops within the dead interval, the others stay FULL, and debug ip ospf adj shows the authentication mismatch. Put it back.
  • show ip ospf neighbor on every core still shows all neighbours FULL.

    The address plan is the ACL
    
VLAN 1xx is always 10.<building>.xx.0/24: CCTV is VLAN 104 and the .4 subnet in every building. That makes one wildcard match every camera at the port, 10.0.4.0 0.255.0.255, and it is why step 8 needs only seven ACLs for five buildings. Each building's /20 (10.10.0.0/20 for administration, 10.20.0.0/20 for the terminal) also means each core could be summarised as one route if the port ever grows to the point where that matters.

  

  
DHCP for every building comes from one server in the data room (10.10.12.10). ip helper-address turns each client's broadcast into a unicast from the SVI's address, which OSPF already knows how to route back. The server keeps one scope per building VLAN.

  

    The passengers are not on this network at all
    
The terminal's passenger and tenant Wi-Fi (thousands of phones a day in summer, the café, the car-rental desks) runs on its own router with its own ISP line, on separate fibre pairs, and is not connected to the ring. Putting it in a VRF across the ring would also work, but it would make the port's routers handle traffic for people with no business there, and an ISP-managed passenger service is what most port operators buy anyway. The cleanest segmentation is no connection.

Each edge advertises a way out only while its own way out works. Fibre is preferred; Starlink waits on the tower roof.

Without the always keyword, default-information originate injects a default only while the router has a default route of its own from something other than this OSPF process. On EDGE1 that is the tracked static: when the ISP stops answering, the track goes down, the static disappears, and the E1 default from EDGE1 is withdrawn. Every core then uses EDGE2's default.

  
Both defaults are type E1, so each core adds its own path cost to the advertised metric. With 10 against 100, EDGE1 always wins while it is alive; with E2 the internal cost would be ignored and only the 10 against 100 would count.     EDGE2's DHCP default needs a distance lower than 110
    
A DHCP-learned default has an administrative distance of 254. EDGE2 also hears EDGE1's default through OSPF at distance 110, so OSPF's route wins, the DHCP default never enters the routing table, and EDGE2 has nothing of its own to originate. It only starts originating after EDGE1 dies and its OSPF default has been withdrawn, which adds seconds to the failover. ip dhcp client default-router distance 100 keeps the Starlink default installed all the time, so EDGE2's E1 default is always in the database, ready to take over.

  

  

    Pin the probe target, and accept that sessions reset
    
The IP SLA probe has a host route to its target through the ISP. Without it, when the fibre default is withdrawn the probe follows OSPF's default out through Starlink, succeeds, and brings the fibre route back up: a flap every few seconds. Each edge also does its own NAT, so an edge failover resets every open connection. For a port that is acceptable: ticketing retries, email reconnects, and nothing important lives in a long TCP session.

Sub-Second Reroute: BFD and SPF Timers

  
Light leaving a fibre is noticed instantly. A neighbour that stops answering through a media converter is not, unless you ask.


  
▲ Show step 6 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  
A cleanly cut fibre takes the port down and OSPF reacts immediately. The failures that hurt are the quiet ones: a media converter that keeps the link up but stops passing frames, a switch that hangs, a unidirectional fibre after a crane catches a duct. OSPF's own dead interval is 40 seconds on these links. BFD detects the same failure in 300 ms and tells OSPF.

  
CORE-ADMIN — BFD on every adjacency, faster SPF
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/1
! 3 x 100 ms: a dead neighbour is declared in 300 ms, not 40 s
 bfd interval 100 min_rx 100 multiplier 3
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/2
 bfd interval 100 min_rx 100 multiplier 3
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/3
 bfd interval 100 min_rx 100 multiplier 3
interface TenGigabitEthernet1/1/4
 bfd interval 100 min_rx 100 multiplier 3
router ospf 1
 bfd all-interfaces
! react to the first change in 50 ms, back off if the ring starts flapping
 timers throttle spf 50 200 5000
 timers throttle lsa 50 200 5000
 timers lsa arrival 100
/routing bfd configuration
add interfaces=sfp-sfpplus1,sfp-sfpplus2,sfp-sfpplus3,sfp-sfpplus4 \
    min-rx=100ms min-tx=100ms multiplier=3
/routing ospf interface-template set [find where !passive] use-bfd=yes

  
The throttle timers decide how quickly a router recomputes after a change: the first SPF runs 50 ms after the trigger, then waits at least 200 ms before the next one, doubling up to 5 s if changes keep coming. That gives a fast first reaction and protection against a flapping link making every router recompute non-stop.

  

    Why not just set the hello timers to one second?
    
ip ospf dead-interval minimal hello-multiplier 4 also detects failure in under a second, but it does it by sending full OSPF hellos every 250 ms on every link, each one processed by the routing process. BFD is a small protocol built for exactly this job, and one BFD session per neighbour can serve OSPF and any other protocol that registers with it. Leave OSPF's timers at their defaults and let BFD do the fast part.

    Stateless ACLs and the word "established"
    
Catalyst SVI ACLs have no connection state. established matches TCP segments with ACK or RST set, which is what replies look like, so CCTV-IN lets cameras answer the NVR's RTSP sessions but not open new connections of their own. UDP replies have no such flag, which is why the cameras' NTP and syslog are permitted by explicit port. On the MikroTik pane the same policy is shorter, because a stateful firewall only has to describe who may start a connection.

  

  

    Why the VTS has no internet path, at all
    
The tower's radar, AIS receivers and VHF recorders are the systems that keep ships from colliding in the approach channel. VTS-IN therefore has two permits and an implicit deny, and NAT-OUT on the edges doesn't include the VTS subnet, so even a mistake in one place can't give the radar a route to the internet.

A core reloads, or a fibre gets spliced, while ferries are boarding. Move the traffic away first.

When a core boots, OSPF can form adjacencies before BFD, the routing table and the line cards are fully programmed, and for a few seconds the ring sends transit traffic through a router that drops it. max-metric router-lsa on-startup 300 makes a freshly booted core advertise its transit links at the maximum metric for five minutes: its own building's networks are still reachable through it, but nobody uses it as a shortcut until it has settled.

/routing ospf interface-template set [find where !passive] cost=65535
# ...do the work, then put the real costs back
/routing ospf interface-template set [find where !passive] cost=10

    max-metric does not hide the router's own networks
    
Stub-router mode raises the cost of a router's links to other routers, not of its connected subnets. That is what we want: the tower's VLANs stay reachable while CORE-TOWER is being drained, and only transit traffic goes round the other way. include-stub would hide the tower's own subnets too, which is only useful when moving them to a different router.

  

  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • Reload CORE-TERM in the lab: for five minutes show ip ospf database router 10.255.0.2 shows its ring links at metric 65535, and traceroutes from ADMIN to CARGO avoid it.
  • Cost-out the ADMIN–CARGO diagonal during a continuous ping between those buildings: no loss.

The Attacker's View

  
Where the residual risk sits once routing and segmentation are in place, and what covers it.


  
    
    
  • Route injection. The classic attack on an OSPF network is joining it: a laptop that speaks OSPF on a user VLAN can advertise a better default and capture traffic. Passive interfaces mean no hello is ever answered on a user VLAN, and SHA-256 authentication plus TTL security protects the ring ports themselves.
    • 
    
  • Physical access to the ring. Ducts run along a public quay and cabinets sit in public areas. A ring fibre spliced to a rogue device still needs the OSPF key, and BFD and adjacency logs make an unexplained link flap visible. Cabinets are locked and alarmed through the access-control system.
    • 
    
  • The key itself. One shared OSPF key on every router means one leaked config is enough. Keep configs out of email and tickets (the archive goes to the syslog server, which only the NOC can read) and rotate the key with the overlapping-lifetime procedure in step 3.
    • 
    
  • Legacy security devices. Cameras, door controllers and fuel PLCs run old firmware for a decade. They are reachable only from their servers, can't start connections, and have no internet path; their servers are where patching effort goes.
    • 
    
  • The single yard uplink. One fibre serves the yard. Its systems fail safe, and the decision is recorded with an owner and a review date, not left implicit.
    • 
    
  • Starlink as a path in. The dish is in bypass mode behind EDGE2's zone firewall with nothing open from outside; CGNAT means there is no inbound reachability anyway.
    •

    What actually changes in the design
    
Forwarding moves into software on the CCR routers, which is fine at port traffic levels but means the ring's throughput is the router's, not a switch ASIC's. There is no stub-router mode, so maintenance relies on the cost-out runbook. And on a mixed network the MikroTik side must set its costs by hand to match what the Cisco side computes, or the two halves of the ring will disagree about the best path.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Route between buildings, switch inside them. Routed /31s turn each building into its own failure domain and let OSPF, not spanning tree, decide paths.
    2. 
      
  2. Silence first, then authenticate. passive-interface default keeps OSPF off every user port; SHA-256 and TTL security protect the ports that remain.
    3. 
      
  3. Let BFD detect, let OSPF compute. Sub-second failover comes from BFD plus throttled SPF, not from aggressive hello timers.
    4. 
      
  4. A default route should follow health. Conditional origination with tracked statics, E1 metrics and the right administrative distance makes failover a property of the routing, not a script.
    5. 
      
  5. Stub areas are about blast radius, not size. One small cabinet gets one route in and gives one route out.
    6. 
      
  6. Plan the maintenance, not just the failure. Stub-router on startup and a cost-out runbook move traffic away before anyone touches a fibre.
