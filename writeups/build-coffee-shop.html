Build: The
Coffee Shop Network

Forty seats, a patio, two tablets running the till, two card terminals and a queue out of the door at 08:30. The network has to stay out of the way. We build it on a single MikroTik router plus one PoE switch in 11 tested steps: card terminals walled off to shrink PCI scope, a click-through guest portal with per-device limits, a 4G backup that only the tills may use, mDNS so the staff tablet finds the music system, and remote management that dials out so it works even behind 4G CGNAT.

This is part 3 of the Build series, and the smallest network in it. That makes it the most common one: almost every café, bakery and small restaurant has these same devices on one flat network, usually the ISP's router with the guest Wi-Fi password on the counter and the card terminal plugged in next to the music box.

    
The design challenge is proportion. The business can't justify enterprise gear or an IT person, but it handles card payments, hosts strangers on its Wi-Fi and loses money every minute the till is offline. One router and one switch, configured carefully, cover all of it.

01

  
The Brief

  
A fictional but typical specialty coffee shop: busy mornings, laptop workers all afternoon, an owner who's never on site at closing.


  
The café seats 40 inside and 16 on the patio. Orders go through two tablets running a cloud POS, a kitchen display tablet at the espresso bar, and a networked receipt printer. Payments go through two standalone card terminals, paired with the acquirer over the internet. There's a music streamer controlled from the tablets, a menu screen, an espresso machine that reports telemetry to its maker, three cameras recording to a cloud NVR, and an office PC for the owner's accounts. Guests want Wi-Fi, and the laptop crowd wants it all afternoon.


  

    
RequirementWhere it comes from, and what it forces

    
      
R1 PaymentsCard terminals handle cardholder data. Isolating them so nothing else can reach them, and they reach only their acquirer, keeps the rest of the network out of PCI DSS scope (subject to the segmentation being tested).

      
R2 Guest Wi-FiGuests accept terms of use before going online, and sessions expire. One guest can't use more than their share.

      
R3 Tills firstA patio full of video calls must never slow down an order or a payment. Guests get at most 60% of the line.

      
R4 OutagesThe fibre goes down a few times a year. Tills and card terminals continue on 4G; guests, camera uploads and IoT don't, because they would eat the data plan in an hour.

      
R5 Music & menuStaff control the music and the menu screen from the POS tablets, even though those devices are on different VLANs.

      
R6 Staff turnoverBaristas change every season. The POS Wi-Fi password never goes on a personal phone, and the guest network can't reach anything that matters anyway.

      
R7 Remote careManaged remotely by an IT contractor, including during a 4G outage behind carrier NAT. No inbound ports open on the router.

      
R8 BudgetNetwork hardware under €1,000. Cabling is quoted separately.

    
  


  
  
02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Small doesn't mean simple. Each of these takes something away to make the result easier to run.


  
1 · The router is also the main access point

  
A hAP ax³ routes, firewalls, runs the hotspot and CAPsMAN, and its own Wi-Fi 6 radio covers the inside. A single outdoor wAP ax covers the patio. There's no controller box and no second indoor AP, so the whole network is two configs plus a switch. The trade-off is that the router sits in the back office where the radio isn't ideal; a 40-seat room is small enough that this is fine. A bigger venue would move the radio out to ceiling APs.


  
2 · Standalone card terminals, in their own VLAN

  
Card terminals that integrate with the till over the LAN (the "ECR" protocol) need a rule from the POS tablets into the payment network. That rule pulls the tablets into PCI scope. Terminals paired with the acquirer through the cloud need nothing from the LAN, so their VLAN can be completely sealed: out to the acquirer, nothing in. The cashier types the amount twice (or the POS pushes it via the cloud), and the network stays simple.


  
3 · A selective backup, not a slow one

  
The usual dual-WAN design throttles everything on the backup. This one refuses whole zones on it: guests, cameras and IoT are rejected on the 4G interface, so the tills keep the full 4G bandwidth and a 20 GB plan lasts days, not hours. Guests see "no internet" during an outage. That's honest, and it keeps the business running.


  
4 · Management dials out

  
A remote-management VPN that listens on the router fails exactly when it's needed: on 4G, the router sits behind carrier-grade NAT and can't receive connections. So R1 is a WireGuard client of the contractor's hub, with a keepalive. It works on fibre, on 4G and behind any NAT, and there's no open port on the router's WAN.

The most important ten lines in the network: the terminals reach their acquirer, and nothing reaches them.


  

    
  


  
R1 — payment VLAN rules (first in the forward chain)
! R1 — C1121X: IOS ACLs can't match host names, so the acquirer's
! published address ranges go into an object-group
object-group network ACQUIRER
 198.51.100.0 255.255.255.0
! read top-down, first match wins; wildcard masks inverted. Deny RFC1918, then permit internet
ip access-list extended V25-IN
 permit udp 10.40.25.0 0.0.0.255 host 10.40.25.1 eq domain
 permit tcp 10.40.25.0 0.0.0.255 object-group ACQUIRER eq 443
 deny   ip any any log
ip access-list extended V25-OUT
 permit tcp any 10.40.25.0 0.0.0.255 established
 deny   ip any any log                          ! nothing starts a connection into payments
interface Vlan25
 ip access-group V25-IN in
 ip access-group V25-OUT out
/ip firewall address-list
add list=ACQUIRER address=pay.acquirer.example comment="from the acquirer's integration guide"
add list=ACQUIRER address=tms.acquirer.example comment="terminal management + updates"
# input = TO the router; forward = THROUGH it; first match wins; end drop = default-deny
/ip firewall filter
add chain=forward action=accept connection-state=established,related,untracked
add chain=forward action=drop connection-state=invalid
add chain=forward action=accept in-interface=v25-pay dst-address-list=ACQUIRER protocol=tcp dst-port=443 \
    comment="terminals -> acquirer only"
add chain=forward action=drop in-interface=v25-pay log=yes log-prefix=PAY-OUT comment="terminals -> anything else"
add chain=forward action=drop out-interface=v25-pay log=yes log-prefix=PAY-IN comment="nothing starts a connection into payments"


  

    Host names in address lists
    
RouterOS resolves a host name in an address list and keeps the dynamic IP entries up to date, re-resolving as the DNS TTL expires. That's exactly right for acquirers that publish host names rather than IP ranges. But it means the firewall depends on R1's own DNS: if a terminal works at opening and fails at noon, look at /ip firewall address-list print where list=ACQUIRER. A CDN-fronted acquirer may have rotated to an address R1 hasn't resolved yet. Ask the acquirer for their published IP ranges if they have them.

  


  

    What this does and doesn't do for PCI DSS
    
Segmentation can take systems out of PCI DSS scope, but only if it actually works, and that has to be tested (the drills in section 16 are a start, and the acquirer's questionnaire will ask). If your terminals integrate with the POS over the LAN, add exactly that one port from the tablets' reserved addresses and accept that the tablets are now in scope. None of this replaces the terminal vendor's own security; it limits what a compromised guest phone or espresso machine could ever reach.

The WAN list stays empty until step 8, so nothing reaches the internet yet. There's deliberately no FastTrack rule: the hAP ax³ easily handles a 300 Mb/s line with queues, and FastTrack would bypass the guest limits in step 7.

Guest Limits: The Tills Come First

  
Each guest device gets 5 Mb/s from the hotspot. All guests together get at most 60% of the line. Nothing else is shaped at all.


  

    
  


  
R1 — total guest cap with a queue tree (on top of the hotspot's per-device queues)
! R1 — one shaper for the whole guest VLAN; per-device fairness is the controller's job
policy-map GUEST-DOWN
 class class-default
  shape average 180000000
  fair-queue
interface Vlan50
 service-policy output GUEST-DOWN
! the 30 Mb/s upload cap is a matching shaper on guest traffic as it egresses the WAN
policy-map GUEST-UP
 class class-default
  shape average 30000000
  fair-queue
/ip firewall mangle
add chain=forward action=mark-packet in-interface=v50-guest new-packet-mark=guest-up passthrough=no
add chain=forward action=mark-packet out-interface=v50-guest new-packet-mark=guest-down passthrough=no
/queue tree
add name=guest-down parent=global packet-mark=guest-down max-limit=180M queue=pcq-download-default
add name=guest-up parent=global packet-mark=guest-up max-limit=30M queue=pcq-upload-default


  
Why a queue tree for the total and not one more simple queue? The hotspot creates a dynamic simple queue per device, and a packet only goes through the first simple queue that matches it. A "total" simple queue would either catch everything (bypassing the per-device limits) or nothing, depending on where it lands in the list. A queue tree is processed independently of simple queues, so each guest packet passes through both: its own 5 Mb/s queue and the shared 180/30 cap.


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • Four guest phones running speed tests together: each tops out near 5 Mb/s.
  • With 40 simulated guests saturating the line (a load generator on the guest VLAN), a POS tablet's card-payment test completes in its normal time, and /queue tree print stats shows guest-down dropping.

The LtAP runs in passthrough mode: it hands its carrier address straight to R1 by DHCP, so R1 sees the 4G link as a plain Ethernet WAN with a DHCP default route at distance 2. The reject rule sits at the very top of the forward chain, above the established/related accept. That way even a connection that was established on fibre is cut the moment its packets start leaving via 4G, so a camera's long-running cloud upload can't keep going over 4G.


  

    Reject, don't drop, on the backup
    
With drop, a guest's phone waits for timeouts and keeps retrying, and some apps retry very aggressively. With reject and "network unreachable", the phone learns immediately that there's no internet and shows it. Also note that 1.1.1.1 is now pinned to the fibre as the probe target, which is why R1's own DNS uses Quad9 (step 2) and not Cloudflare.

  • Unplug the fibre: within ~15–20 s the POS tablets and terminals work again (via 4G). A guest phone reports "no internet", the camera uploads stop, and the counter on the NO4G reject rule rises (/ip firewall filter print stats).
  • Plug the fibre back: everything returns to fibre and guests come back online.

    The callback port is vendor-specific
    
Discovery is only half of it. Several music systems also open connections from the speaker back to the app to push "now playing" updates. Without that rule, the app finds the speaker and plays, but its controls freeze after a minute. Port 3400 is one vendor's choice. The Attacker's View

  
Who would attack a café? Mostly opportunists: someone at a table with a laptop, or someone who walks in to swap a card terminal.


  
    
    
  • The evil twin. Anyone can broadcast "CAFE-Guest" from a laptop and catch guests whose phones join it. The network can't prevent that; it's the reason an open guest SSID must never be the way staff devices get online, and why the POS uses a WPA3 network with a password.
    • 
    
  • Terminal swap or skimmer overlay. The real card-fraud risk in hospitality is physical. Record the terminals' serial numbers, check the tamper seals at opening, and notice when a terminal's MAC changes: the DHCP reservation means a swapped device gets no address and the till stops taking cards, which is noisy on purpose.
    • 
    
  • A cable under the counter. The counter switch is within reach of a customer leaning over. Every port has a fixed purpose, and the payment ports only lead to the acquirer anyway. Disable anything unused, and consider locking the switch in the back office with only the terminal cables running out.
    • 
    
  • The espresso machine. Connected appliances get old firmware and few updates. In VLAN 30 it can reach its maker's cloud and nothing local except the speaker rules, which is the worst a compromised machine can do here.
    • 
    
  • A laptop hogging or attacking the guest network. Client isolation stops guest-to-guest attacks; the per-device queue stops one laptop from eating the bandwidth; the 2-hour session limit stops a "free office" from running all day on one login.
    •

    What actually changes in the design
    
Host names become address ranges. The payment allowlist is the one place where Cisco is less convenient: IOS ACLs match addresses, so you need the acquirer's published ranges and must update them when the acquirer changes provider. The portal moves to the Wi-Fi controller, which is actually the more natural place for it. One box replaces three, at about four times the price, with a support contract behind it.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Seal the payment VLAN first. Out to the acquirer's host names, nothing in, and test it. That empty column in the matrix is the PCI argument.
    2. 
      
  2. Standalone terminals keep the network simpler than LAN-integrated ones, because nothing local needs a path into payments.
    3. 
      
  3. A click-through hotspot with per-device queues covers terms of use, fairness and session limits in a few lines. A queue tree, not another simple queue, caps the total.
    4. 
      
  4. Make the backup selective. Reject guests, cameras and IoT on 4G, at the top of the chain, so the tills keep working and the data plan survives.
    5. 
      
  5. mDNS repeating finds devices; the firewall still decides who may connect. Allow the vendor's callback port from one address, not VLAN to VLAN.
    6. 
      
  6. Let the router dial out for management. It works behind CGNAT, and there's nothing to attack on the WAN.
