Build: The
Accounting Office Network

A 24-person accounting firm in Heraklion that holds the tax files, payroll and bank details of a few hundred businesses, and doubles its pace every spring. This is part 14 of the Build series. The law firm and the clinic each turned on 802.1X in one step; here it is the whole design. The office is small enough that one Windows server does everything, and that is the constraint that shapes every choice: NPS as RADIUS, an enterprise CA for the certificates, and an honest plan for what NPS can't do.

    
The order is the lesson. Build the network static, introduce identity in monitor mode, enforce it, and only then write the firewall rules between the VLANs that identity now fills.

The Brief

  
A fictional but typical firm: three partners, 21 staff, about 400 business clients, two floors above a shop in the city centre.


  
The firm keeps its clients' tax filings, payroll, accounts and bank details on one Windows server in a comms cupboard, with the accounting software on a second server that seasonal staff use through Remote Desktop. Partners handle payroll exports and the firm's own finances. From March to May six seasonal accountants join on loaner laptops. Clients drop by all day and wait in the meeting room. Everything has to keep working through the tax-season deadlines, when an hour of downtime is an hour of late filings.

02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
All four start from the server the firm already has.


  
1 · Certificates, not passwords

  
Every firm PC gets a machine certificate from the firm's own CA through auto-enrolment, and authenticates with EAP-TLS before anyone logs in. Personal devices can't get a certificate, so R1 holds without asking anyone to type anything.


  
2 · The VLAN follows the identity

  
NPS returns the VLAN with the authorisation: partner PCs to 10, staff to 20, seasonal laptops to 25, printers to 40. Every desk socket is the same, and the firewall rules are written per VLAN, so the identity decision and the access decision are made in two simple places.


  
3 · Monitor first, then closed

  
IBNS 2.0 policies run the same authentication in open mode and closed mode; only one line differs. A week in open mode finds every device that would have been locked out, while nobody is.


  
4 · When NPS is down, fail into a small room

  
Critical authentication puts new sessions into a VLAN that reaches only the domain controller and the accounting share, both of which still ask for credentials. Work continues; a stranger gets nothing usable.

SW2 is the same template without the server ports, uplinked to SW1's Gi1/1/2. The desk ports start in STAFF (VLAN 20) on purpose: nobody notices the switches going in, and the real decision about which VLAN a desk lands in moves to NPS in step 6.

  

    DHCP snooping is the quiet prerequisite
    
DHCP snooping builds the binding table of which MAC holds which IP on which port, and blocks rogue DHCP servers on untrusted ports. 802.1X doesn't need it to authenticate anyone, but the session table, accounting and anything that later wants to know a client's IP rely on that table or on device-tracking. Turning it on in step 1, while every port is still static, means its effect can be checked before identity is layered on top.

  • A laptop running a DHCP server on a desk port: show ip dhcp snooping statistics counts its offers as dropped.

Small office, one router: every VLAN's gateway is a subinterface on R1, so every packet between VLANs passes the firewall in step 10.

07 · Step 3 of 11

  
Identity: A CA, NPS and Auto-Enrolled Certificates

  
The decision of who is who lives on the domain controller: a certificate authority that hands every firm PC a certificate, and NPS that checks it.


  
▲ Show step 3 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  
The office already runs a Windows domain for its accounting software and file shares. That makes the natural RADIUS server NPS (Network Policy Server, free with Windows Server), and the natural identity a machine certificate issued by an enterprise CA and auto-enrolled to every domain-joined PC through Group Policy. A PC proves it belongs to the firm by holding a private key the firm's CA signed. There is no password for anyone to phish, share or type into a personal laptop.

  
DC1 — PowerShell · enterprise CA, auto-enrolment template, NPS and its RADIUS clients
# on DC1 (Windows Server 2025): domain controller, DHCP, DNS, NTP — and now CA and NPS
Install-WindowsFeature ADCS-Cert-Authority, NPAS -IncludeManagementTools
Install-AdcsCertificationAuthority -CAType EnterpriseRootCA -CACommonName "OFFICE-ROOT-CA" `
    -KeyLength 4096 -HashAlgorithmName SHA256 -ValidityPeriod Years -ValidityPeriodUnits 10
# duplicate the 'Workstation Authentication' template as OFFICE-MACHINE:
#   Domain Computers: Read + Enroll + Autoenroll; publish it; then a GPO on the PCs:
#   Computer Config > Windows Settings > Security Settings > Public Key Policies >
#   Certificate Services Client - Auto-Enrollment = Enabled (renew, update)
Add-CATemplate -Name "OFFICE-MACHINE" -Force
# NPS needs its own server certificate (RAS and IAS Server template) and to sit in AD
netsh nps add registeredserver
New-NpsRadiusClient -Name SW1 -Address 10.60.99.11 -SharedSecret '********'
New-NpsRadiusClient -Name SW2 -Address 10.60.99.12 -SharedSecret '********'
New-NpsRadiusClient -Name EWC -Address 10.60.99.20 -SharedSecret '********'

    Why not PEAP with the user's password?
    
PEAP-MSCHAPv2 is quicker to set up and it is still what many offices run. It is also crackable offline from a captured exchange, and it lets any device that knows a staff password onto the network, including a personal laptop at home that will be brought in during tax season. EAP-TLS with a machine certificate ties access to a device the firm owns and manages, and the key never leaves it (put it in the TPM with the template's Key Storage Provider setting).

  

  

    NPS can do MAB, but only one awkward way
    
NPS has no MAC address list. A MAB request arrives as a PAP login whose user name and password are both the device's MAC, so NPS checks it against Active Directory: each phone and printer needs a user account named after its MAC, with the MAC as its password, in a group the policy matches. Put those accounts in their own OU, deny them interactive logon, and expect a fine-grained password policy to be needed because a 12-hex-digit password fails the domain's complexity rules. It works for ten printers and phones; at a hundred, it is the reason people buy ISE or ClearPass.

  

  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step

  • NPS console: three RADIUS clients, six policies in this order; the NPS server certificate is selected in the EAP-TLS settings of policies 1 to 3.

The attribute lines make the switches send what NPS and later investigations need: the calling MAC in every request (attribute 31, the MAC, is sent by default; 8 adds the client's IP once it is known; 25 echoes the Class that NPS returned when a session re-authenticates), and Cisco VSAs so NPS can return device-traffic-class=voice in step 7.

  

    Write the local user before aaa new-model
    
aaa new-model takes effect immediately, including on the console and the VTY lines. With no local user and aaa authentication login default local, the next login fails. The order in the block above is the safe one: local account, then aaa new-model, then the RADIUS server. Keep the SSH session open and test a second login before closing it.

  

  

    automate-tester is what makes critical auth recover
    
When NPS stops answering, the switch marks it dead for deadtime minutes and then tries again only when a real request arrives. With automate-tester it sends its own test login for a non-existent user (radius-probe), every minute with idle-time 1 (the default is an hour); an Access-Reject proves NPS is alive and raises the aaa-available event that step 8 depends on.

Turn 802.1X on everywhere, on a Friday, and change nothing that anybody will notice on Monday.

Closed Mode: The VLAN Follows the Identity

  
One line per port turns the inventory into enforcement. From now on, the certificate decides the VLAN.

SW1 and SW2 — closed mode, parking VLAN, server-driven reauth
interface range GigabitEthernet1/0/1 - 20
! an authenticated session with no VLAN from NPS lands in PARKING, not in STAFF
 switchport access vlan 999
! from now on, nothing but EAPOL until the session is authorised

! re-check every session when NPS's Session-Timeout says so (8 h)
 authentication periodic
 authentication timer reauthenticate server
# closed: a failure now lands in PARKING (999), which goes nowhere

# VLANs arrive from NPS (Tunnel-Private-Group-ID); vlan-filtering must be on
/interface bridge set bridge vlan-filtering=yes

  
In closed mode a port passes nothing but EAPOL until the session is authorised, then puts it in the VLAN NPS returned: 10 for a partner's PC, 20 for staff. The same wall socket gives a different network depending on whose machine is plugged in, so hot desks during tax season need no repatching.

  
The configured access VLAN changes meaning: it is now what a session gets if NPS authorises it without naming a VLAN, which only happens if a policy is misconfigured. Pointing it at PARKING turns that mistake into "no access" rather than "staff access".

  

    An unknown laptop gets nothing, not a guest VLAN
    
Older designs send failures to a guest or auth-fail VLAN. Here a laptop that fails 802.1X and MAB gets nothing at all, and the policy retries every 60 seconds so the port recovers by itself once the device is enrolled. Clients who need internet use the MEETING Wi-Fi (step 9), not a wall socket in an office full of client files.

  

  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • A personal laptop on a desk port: no link-local traffic gets through, show access-session shows it failing MAB, NPS logs DENY-ALL.
  • A partner's laptop on the same port: VLAN 10, an address from 10.60.10.0/24.

    A MAC is not an identity
    
Anyone can read a printer's MAC off its label and set it on a laptop. That is why PRINT is its own VLAN with the narrowest rules in the office (step 10): a spoofed printer reaches the file server's scan folder over SMB and nothing else. Printers also get a DHCP reservation, and an alert on a second device with the same MAC is cheap to add on the DHCP server.

  

  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step

When NPS Is Down in April: Critical Auth

  
A RADIUS server that reboots for updates on 30 April must not stop thirty people filing tax returns.


  
▲ Show step 8 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  
Without this step, a session that needs to authenticate while NPS is unreachable fails, and the port stays closed. That is the right answer for a bank and the wrong one for an office whose whole year depends on six weeks. Critical authentication decides what happens instead.

  
SW1 and SW2 — critical VLAN, voice, and recovery when NPS returns
service-template CRIT-DATA
 vlan 60
class-map type control subscriber match-all AAA_DOWN_UNAUTHD
 match result-type aaa-timeout
 match authorization-status unauthorized
class-map type control subscriber match-all AAA_DOWN_AUTHD
 match result-type aaa-timeout
 match authorization-status authorized
class-map type control subscriber match-any IN_CRIT
 match activated-service-template CRIT-DATA
 match activated-service-template DEFAULT_CRITICAL_VOICE_TEMPLATE
policy-map type control subscriber OFFICE-NAC
 event authentication-failure match-first
! NPS unreachable, new device: CRIT VLAN for data, voice keeps working
  10 class AAA_DOWN_UNAUTHD do-until-failure
   10 activate service-template CRIT-DATA
   20 activate service-template DEFAULT_CRITICAL_VOICE_TEMPLATE
   30 authorize
   40 pause reauthentication
! NPS unreachable at reauth time: keep what it already granted
  15 class AAA_DOWN_AUTHD do-until-failure
   10 pause reauthentication
   20 authorize
 event aaa-available match-all
! NPS is back: throw out the critical sessions so they authenticate properly
  10 class IN_CRIT do-until-failure
   10 clear-session
  20 class always do-until-failure
   10 resume reauthentication
# NPS unreachable: new sessions go to CRIT (60) instead of being rejected
/interface dot1x server set [find] server-fail-vlan-id=60

  
Sessions that were already authorised before NPS went away keep their VLAN when their reauthentication fails. New sessions, a PC that was rebooted or a laptop that arrived, go to CRIT (VLAN 60), which can reach only the domain controller and the accounting file server. Phones keep their voice VLAN. When the probe from step 4 gets an answer again, clear-session throws every critical session out so it authenticates properly.

  

    Critical means anyone, so CRIT must be small
    
While NPS is down the switch can't tell a firm PC from a stranger: everyone who connects gets CRIT. That is why CRIT reaches two servers and no internet, and why both servers still require a domain login; a stranger in CRIT finds a domain controller that asks for Kerberos and a share that asks for credentials. A second NPS on another host would make this rare, and is the first upgrade worth buying.

  

  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • Stop the NPS service, reboot a staff PC: it comes up in 10.60.60.x, opens the accounting share, and can't browse the web.
  • A PC that was already up keeps VLAN 20 through the outage.
  • Start NPS: within about a minute show access-session shows nothing left in CRIT; the rebooted PC is back in VLAN 20.

When someone leaves on bad terms, don't wait: disable the computer account, then clear access-session interface on their port and the session re-authenticates and fails at once.

The Attacker's View

  
Where the residual risk sits once every port asks for a certificate.


  
    
    
  • MAB is the soft spot. Phones and printers authenticate by MAC, and a MAC is printed on the device. PRINT and VOICE are the two narrowest VLANs in the matrix for exactly that reason.
    • 
    
  • Behind an authorised port. An attacker with a tap or a small hub between a firm PC and the wall can ride the PC's session in multi-domain mode. MACsec would close it; on this budget, locked desks and cable management are the control, and the switch logs a second MAC on a data domain.
    • 
    
  • The certificate is the identity. A stolen laptop carries a valid certificate until its computer account is disabled. Report-a-lost-laptop is a five-minute procedure: disable the account, revoke the certificate, clear the session.
    • 
    
  • Critical auth. During an NPS outage anyone gets CRIT. It reaches two servers that still demand domain credentials, and the outage itself raises an alarm (step 11). A second NPS would shrink this window to almost nothing.
    • 
    
  • One server does everything. DC, CA and NPS on one Windows host is a single target. It is patched first, backed up off-site, has no internet browsing, and its admin accounts are separate from daily ones.

    What actually changes in the design
    
There is no monitor mode, so the rollout uses the reject-VLAN trick instead and the inventory comes from the dot1x log. Critical sessions don't clear themselves when NPS returns; schedule a script that resets them, or accept that they stay in CRIT until the next reauthentication. Check the dot1x properties used in the MikroTik panes against your RouterOS version, as several were added during the 7.x series.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Machine certificates beat passwords. EAP-TLS with auto-enrolment admits the firm's devices and nothing else, with nothing for a user to type or share.
    2. 
      
  2. Let identity choose the VLAN, then firewall the VLANs. Two simple decisions in two places are easier to get right than one complicated one.
    3. 
      
  3. Roll out in open mode. A week of authenticating without enforcing finds every forgotten device while nobody is locked out.
    4. 
      
  4. Decide what "RADIUS is down" means before it happens. A small critical VLAN keeps the office working without letting strangers in.
    5. 
      
  5. Know what your RADIUS server can't do. NPS has no CoA and awkward MAB; a reauthentication timer and a manual clear access-session cover the gap.
    6. 
      
  6. Seasonal access should end by itself. Expiring accounts and an RDS-only VLAN make the end of the season a non-event.
