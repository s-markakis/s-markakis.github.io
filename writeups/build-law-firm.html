The Brief

  
Before any switch is chosen, write down who works there, what they handle, and what would hurt them. The design comes from this list.


  
The client is a fictional but typical Greek commercial-law practice. There are four partners, eight associates, eight paralegals, two secretaries, a receptionist, two people in accounts, and three trainees: 28 people across two floors of a renovated building. The firm runs a document management system (DMS) that holds every matter file, a Windows domain for identity, hosted VoIP, two multifunction printers that scan to folders, and a CCTV system with door access. Clients come in for meetings and expect Wi-Fi. Partners work from home two days a week and reach court e-filing portals from wherever they are.


  

    
RequirementWhere it comes from, and what it forces in the design

    
      
R1 ConfidentialityProfessional secrecy and GDPR Art. 32. Client files must be reachable only by people with a reason to see them, so the servers sit in their own zone behind a default-deny policy.

      
R2 VisitorsClients and opposing counsel sit in meeting rooms with laptops, so the guest network must have no route into the firm, not just a firewall rule.

R6 RansomwareLaw firms are a favourite ransomware target because they pay. Backups must be unreachable from user machines and immutable offsite.

      
R7 VoiceHosted VoIP has to stay clear while someone uploads a 2 GB evidence bundle, so voice gets priority queuing at the WAN edge.

      
R8 Small ITThe firm has no IT staff; an outside contractor (us) manages it. The design must be boring, documented, and remotely manageable, with nothing clever that needs a specialist.

    
  


  

    The ethical wall is not a VLAN
    
Law firms sometimes have to build an ethical wall (also called a "Chinese wall") between two teams, for example when one partner's former client is now on the other side of a matter. It is tempting to do that in the network with a VLAN per practice group. Don't. Matter-level access belongs in the DMS's own permissions, where it follows the person and the file. The network's job is coarser: keep categories of device apart (people, servers, guests, cameras). Trying to model which lawyer can see which matter with ACLs gives you an unmanageable policy that the first reorganisation of practice groups will break.

  


  
  
02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Most of the design is forced by the brief. These four decisions aren't, and each one is a trade-off worth stating out loud.


  
1 · Collapsed core, not three-tier

  
With 28 users, two floors and about 60 ports, one L3 core switch with two access switches is plenty (see the topology guide). The core is a single point of failure, and we accept that on purpose: a cold-spare C9200 on the shelf with the config backed up restores service in 20 minutes, while a stacked or redundant core would double the switching budget to protect against a failure that happens perhaps once a decade. The brief's real availability risk is the WAN (R5), so that's where the redundancy money goes.


  
2 · Inter-VLAN routing on the core, policy on the SVIs

  
Inter-VLAN routing could happen on the core switch, at line rate, with stateless ACLs. Or it could happen on R1's zone-based firewall, which is stateful but sends all east-west traffic through a branch router. A C1111 handles a few hundred Mbps with ZBF enabled, while a DMS sync or a scan-to-folder burst easily fills a 1G port. So routing and ACLs live on the core, the stateful firewall guards the internet edge, and step 5 deals honestly with the cost of stateless ACLs.


  
3 · The guest VLAN has no SVI on the core

  
VLAN 50 (guest) is carried through the core at Layer 2 only and terminates on R1. The core has no address in the guest subnet, so it can never route guest traffic directly into another VLAN. Every guest packet has to go through R1, where it meets the only stateful firewall in the building, with no GUEST→INSIDE zone pair (drop by default). As a second control, an ACL on the core's transit interface drops anything sourced from the guest subnet. A guest reaches a client file only if both of those are wrong at the same time. Step 7 shows why the second control matters.


  
4 · Identity decides the VLAN, not the wall socket

  
Every desk port starts in a dead "parking" VLAN. A machine that passes 802.1X (domain machine certificate, checked by NPS on the domain controller) is placed in VLAN 10 or 20 according to its AD group. Phones and printers, which can't do 802.1X, fall back to MAC Authentication Bypass (MAB). Anything else gets nothing. Moving a lawyer to a different office then needs no switch change at all, which matters for R8.

Three cells are worth explaining because they are where real networks go wrong:

  
    
    
  • Print → files (scan-to-folder). The MFP has to initiate SMB to FS01. If you lock print down to "replies only", scanning breaks on day one and someone "fixes" it by opening VLAN 45 to everything. Allow exactly TCP 445 to FS01 and nothing else.
    • 
    
  • Staff → NVR. Only the reception PC (one /32) may view cameras. The rest of the staff VLAN is denied, because CCTV footage of who visits a law firm is itself confidential.
    • 
    
  • Servers → Internet. Servers may start outbound HTTPS only (updates, offsite backup). A compromised server's first move is a callback to its controller, and blocking everything except 443 to the internet keeps that out of easy reach.

The build

  
Bottom to Top, One Layer at a Time

  
Build it the way you'd troubleshoot it: start where the users plug in and work up towards the internet, and don't move up until the current layer passes its checkpoint.

  
Everything above was planning. From here we build in 13 steps, starting with the endpoints and access switches, then the wireless, the core, the servers, the policy on the core, the edge router, the two ISPs, and finally the remote users. Each step opens with the same topology: what's already built is solid, what this step adds is highlighted in orange, and what comes later is faded. Each step ends with a checkpoint. If a checkpoint fails, the fault is in the layer you just added, because everything below it has already been tested.

  
Test-PC tip: Windows Firewall blocks inbound ping by default. Enable the "File and Printer Sharing (Echo Request – ICMPv4-In)" rule on the test PCs, or every ping checkpoint below will fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the network.

  
Building bottom-up also means each layer's tests can't be confused by the layers above. When a lawyer's PC can't reach the DMS at step 4, there's no firewall, NAT or VPN yet that could be the cause.

    Why cameras get sticky MACs
    
CCTV cameras are mounted on the outside of the building, at the entrance, where anyone with a ladder can reach the cable. Unplug the camera, plug in a laptop, and on a typical SMB network you are on the LAN. Here the sticky MAC shuts the port down the moment a different MAC shows up, and VLAN 60 gets no route anywhere once step 5 applies its ACL. This is the cheapest physical-intrusion control in the whole post.

    Three ways this failover lies to you
    
1. The probe escapes. If the fibre interface goes down, the pinned /32 disappears with it, the probe to 1.1.1.1 follows the 5G default, gets answered, and track 1 comes back up while the fibre is still dead. The Null0 route at AD 250 stops that. 2. Stale NAT. Existing translations still point at the old outside address, so long-lived sessions (Teams, VoIP registrations) hang until they time out. The EEM applet clears the table on every state change. 3. The VPN goes away. The 5G provider uses CGNAT, so while on backup nobody can reach R1 from outside. Remote lawyers drop until the fibre is back. That is a documented, accepted limitation: if the firm needs remote access during a fibre outage, the fix is a 5G plan with a public IP, not a cleverer config.

    Add a self-zone policy and your failover breaks
    
Without an OUTSIDE→self zone pair, traffic to the router itself is allowed. As soon as you add one (and you need it, to restrict the VPN), anything not listed is dropped, including the echo replies to R1's own IP SLA probes. Traffic the router originates isn't inspected, so there is no state entry to let the reply back in. Track 1 goes down and stays down, and the site sits on 5G with a perfectly healthy fibre. That's why the echo-reply and DHCP lines are in OUT-SELF. Whenever you change a self-zone policy, run show track 1 immediately afterwards.

  

  

    Why full tunnel is right here
    
Split tunnelling sends only firm-bound traffic through the VPN and everything else straight out of the hotel's network. That is lighter on the firm's 100 Mb/s upload. But a lawyer's laptop carrying privileged documents would then browse, download and fetch email attachments over a network the firm doesn't control, without any of its controls applied. With 12 lawyers, 3–4 concurrent tunnels is the realistic peak and the upload has room for it. If it ever doesn't, a cloud-delivered web filter on the laptop is a better answer than split tunnelling.

Backups Ransomware Can't Reach

  
If a firm loses its client files, it has a problem that lawyers, not IT, will be dealing with. The design rule is that no user machine can reach any copy of the backups.


  

    
  


  
The network contributes three things. (1) No user VLAN has a rule allowing traffic to NAS01; the only path to it is from inside VLAN 30. (2) The NAS pulls from the servers using its own read-only service account, so a compromised server doesn't have credentials that can write to the NAS. (3) Offsite copies go out over 443 to object storage with object lock, so not even an attacker holding the NAS admin password can delete them early. The NAS admin account is not a domain account, and no routed VLAN except management (99) can reach its web UI. The quarterly test restore (the "0") is on the contractor's calendar, and the result is written down.

The Attacker's View

  
I test networks like this for a living. Here is what I'd still try against this one, and why each item is a decision rather than an oversight.


  
    
    
  • Phishing a lawyer, then using the DMS as that lawyer. The network can't stop this: the stolen session uses the same TCP 443 to DMS01 that the real lawyer uses. The answer is MFA on the DMS, DMS audit logs, and alerts on bulk exports. Segmentation limits how far the attacker gets; it doesn't stop the first compromise.
    • 
    
  • The domain controller is reachable by everyone. permit ip … host 10.20.30.10 is in every user ACL, because AD needs it. That makes DC01 the main target (Kerberoasting, coercion, relay). The defences are AD hygiene, not the network: LAPS, tiered admin accounts, SMB signing, and no domain admin ever logging on to a workstation.
    • 
    
  • MFP "pass-back". Multifunction printers store an SMB account for scan-to-folder and an LDAP bind account for the address book. Anyone who can reach the printer's web UI can point the LDAP server at their own laptop, click "test", and receive the bind password. Here the MFP web UI is reachable only from VLAN 99 (users get raw printing on port 9100 only), the admin password is changed from the default, and the scan account can only write to \\FS01\Scans.
    • 
    
  • Spoofing a phone's MAC. Copy a phone's MAC from its label, set it on a laptop, and MAB puts you in VLAN 40. You get DNS, NTP and the internet, but no internal hosts. That's a deliberately small payoff for a known weakness of MAB.
    • 
    
  • Moving within one VLAN. The switches never see lawyer-to-lawyer traffic. The Windows Firewall GPO from step 5 is the control there, and it gets tested on every visit, not just assumed to be in place.

    What actually changes in the design
    
Routing moves to the router. A CRS can route between VLANs in hardware, but not with a firewall inspecting every packet, and this design depends on that inspection. So the core becomes a pure Layer 2 switch, and the CCR2004 routes and filters everything. Its inter-VLAN capacity (several Gb/s with a firewall) is plenty for 28 people. The firewall becomes stateful. The two biggest gotchas of the Cisco build disappear: no established flags, and no RADIUS-reply line that silently breaks 802.1X. Connection tracking handles every reply. The guest pattern gets simpler. With all routing on R1, "no SVI on the core" is automatic, and the V900-IN backstop becomes one drop rule for the GUEST interface list.

    
What you give up: per-port "PC behind phone" authentication is simpler on Cisco (RouterOS authenticates the port, so the phone rides the tagged voice VLAN while the PC authenticates), there's no Dynamic ARP Inspection, and WireGuard has no per-user RADIUS/MFA of its own. For a 28-person firm that's a reasonable trade for a fifth of the price; for a regulated firm that must show per-user VPN logs, keep IKEv2 with EAP.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Start from the brief, not the topology. Eight requirements produced every decision in this post. A design you can't trace back to a requirement is decoration.
    2. 
      
  2. The policy matrix is the security design. The ACLs and zone pairs are just that table written in IOS. Review the table with the client; nobody reviews ACLs.
    3. 
      
  3. Build bottom-up and test every layer before going up. When a checkpoint fails, the fault is in the layer you just added.
    4. 
      
  4. Make guests go through the firewall, then back it up. No SVI on the core forces every guest packet through R1's zone firewall, and V900-IN on the core is an independent second control.
    5. 
      
  5. 802.1X moves identity from the wall socket to the machine, and the fail-open versus fail-closed choice for when NPS is down is a business decision. Write it down.
    6. 
      
  6. Stateless SVI ACLs are fast but not smart. Every UDP reply needs its own line (the RADIUS outage), and they do nothing within a VLAN. Host firewalls cover what the switch can't see.
    7. 
      
  7. Dual-WAN fails in subtle ways: NAT stays tied to one interface without route-maps, an unpinned probe "recovers" through the backup, and a self-zone policy silently breaks the probe replies. Test with a real fibre pull.
    8. 
      
  8. Backups are a network-design problem too. The NAS pulls from the servers, no user VLAN can reach it, and offsite storage is immutable. A restore that has never been timed hasn't been tested.
