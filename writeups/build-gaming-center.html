Forty gaming PCs, six console booths, a counter and a lounge full of waiting parents. This is part 2 of the Build series. The law firm was about keeping trusted staff away from things they didn't need. A gaming center turns that around: the people sitting at the keyboards are strangers, some of them are bored teenagers with a USB stick, and a few of them will try to cheat, sniff or crash the person next to them. At the same time the business sells exactly one thing, a fast and smooth game, so every security control has to cost zero latency.

    
It's built on MikroTik because that's what most real gaming centers run: a CCR handles 1 Gb/s of NAT with queues on every packet for a few hundred euros, and the CRS3xx switch chip can isolate ports at line rate. The design ideas carry over to any vendor.

The Brief

  
A fictional but typical center: one hall, 40 PCs, 6 console booths, open 14 hours a day, and an owner who measures the network in complaints per evening.


  
The arena has 40 gaming PCs in four rows of ten, the first row beside a small stage used for weekend tournaments. There are 6 console booths with a PS5 and a big TV each, a counter with the POS PC and a card terminal, a lounge where parents wait and want Wi-Fi, and 8 cameras. Customers pay per hour through a cloud session-management service that runs a client on every PC. The owner wants three things above all: nobody complains about lag, nobody can mess with the PC next to them, and nobody leaves anything behind on a PC.


  

    
RequirementWhere it comes from, and what it forces

    
      
R1 Clean seatsCustomers install things, plug in USB sticks and leave logged-in accounts behind. Every seat must be factory-clean after every reboot, so the seats are diskless and boot an image over the network.

      
R2 Seat isolationCustomers attack each other: ARP poisoning to snoop logins, LAN flooding to lag a rival. Seats must not reach each other at Layer 2, which means isolation in the switch, not on the router.

      
R3 Latency firstOne customer downloading a 120 GB game must not add a single millisecond to someone else's match. Interactive traffic is queued ahead of bulk on every packet.

      
R4 Download onceForty seats patching the same game on release day would saturate any line. A LAN game cache fetches each title once and serves it at 10G.

      
R5 Consoles need open NATPS5 matchmaking and voice chat suffer behind strict NAT. Consoles get UPnP; nothing else does.

      
R6 Stay online, stay stableTwo ISPs. But a game that switches public IP mid-match drops anyway, so games stay on WAN1 and only bulk downloads use WAN2, until something fails.

      
R7 TournamentsOn weekends, seats 1–10 must see each other for LAN play, then go back to isolated. That has to be one action for staff, not a config change.

      
R8 Money & camerasThe card terminal and POS are on their own network, and so are the cameras. Neither is ever reachable from a seat.

    
  


  
  
02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Most of this is forced by the brief. These four choices aren't, and each one is a trade-off worth stating out loud.


  
1 · Diskless seats, served at Layer 2

  
Diskless platforms boot every seat from a golden image on a server over iSCSI; each seat's writes go to an overlay that's thrown away at reboot. That solves R1 completely. The network decision is where the boot server sits. When a full room powers on at opening time, every seat reads several gigabytes at once. If that traffic had to cross the router, the router would become the bottleneck and the opening queue would last ten minutes. So BOOT01 sits in the seats' VLAN, on a 10G port of the seat switch, and the boot storm never leaves the switch chip.


  
2 · Isolation in the switch chip, not in VLANs

  
The textbook way to stop clients talking to each other is a VLAN per client or private VLANs. Forty VLANs would mean forty subnets, forty DHCP scopes and a boot server with forty legs. Instead, the CRS354's switch chip does port isolation: each seat port may only forward to the uplink and the two server ports. Seats share one subnet, can't exchange a single frame, and the boot and cache servers are still one hop away at line rate.


  
3 · Games pinned to WAN1, bulk on WAN2

  
Load-balancing every connection across two WANs (PCC) sounds efficient, but games and anti-cheat systems dislike a player whose public IP changes between connections, and some platforms flag it. So interactive traffic always uses WAN1, and only the game cache's downloads use WAN2, where a slow line doesn't matter. If either WAN fails, everything moves to the other one.


  
4 · No FastTrack

  
FastTrack makes RouterOS much faster by skipping most of the packet path, including the queues. That would defeat R3. A CCR2004 handles 1 Gb/s of NAT with a queue tree and full firewall without FastTrack, so we leave it off and accept the extra CPU load. On smaller routers, this trade-off goes the other way.

Seats & the Seat Switch

  
Forty seats on one CRS354, one VLAN, and a switch chip that refuses to forward a single frame from one seat to another.


  
▲ Show step 1 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  
The CRS354-48G-4S+2Q+ has 48 gigabit ports for the seats and four 10G SFP+ ports: one up to the router and two for the game servers, with one spare. All seat ports are untagged members of VLAN 10. The uplink is a tagged trunk. Hardware offload stays on, so the switch chip does all of this at line rate, not the switch's CPU.

Now the isolation. On CRS3xx switches, each port can be given an explicit list of ports it's allowed to forward to. A seat port may forward only to the uplink and the two server ports:


  
SW-SEATS — port isolation in the switch chip
! SW-SEATS — Catalyst 9300-48T + C9300-NM-8X: protected ports are Cisco's port isolation
interface range GigabitEthernet1/0/1 - 40
 description SEAT
 switchport mode access
 switchport access vlan 10
 switchport protected                     ! protected ports never forward to each other
 spanning-tree portfast
 spanning-tree bpduguard enable
interface range TenGigabitEthernet1/1/1 - 3
 description R1 uplink / BOOT01 / CACHE01  ! NOT protected: every seat reaches these
! careful: an unprotected port can reach every protected one (see tournament mode)
:for i from=1 to=40 do={
  /interface ethernet switch port-isolation add port=("ether" . $i) \
      forwarding-override=sfp-sfpplus1,sfp-sfpplus2,sfp-sfpplus3
}


  

    Why not just firewall seat-to-seat on the router?
    
Two seats in the same subnet never send their traffic to the router. Seat 12 ARPs for seat 13, gets its MAC and sends frames straight across the switch. A router firewall rule can't see that traffic, and that's exactly where ARP poisoning and LAN floods happen. The block has to be in the switch. Port isolation does it in hardware, which also means it can't be bypassed by changing an IP address. The port isolation menu has moved between RouterOS releases, so check /interface ethernet switch port-isolation print on yours, and confirm the bridge ports still show the H (hardware-offloaded) flag afterwards.

  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • /interface bridge port print: every port has the H flag (hardware offload).
  • /interface bridge vlan print: VLAN 10 lists ether1–40 as (dynamic) untagged and sfp-sfpplus1 as tagged.
  • Two seats with temporary static addresses 10.30.10.201 and .202 can't ping each other, and arp -a on each shows no entry for the other.
  • Remove the isolation entry for one of them: the ping works. Put it back.

The Game Servers: Boot & Cache

  
Two boxes on 10G in the seats' own VLAN. One serves every seat's operating system, the other serves every game download after the first.


  
▲ Show step 2 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  
BOOT01 runs the diskless platform: an NVMe-backed golden Windows image plus a writeback overlay per seat. Its 10G port is untagged in VLAN 10 (address 10.30.10.10) and tagged in VLAN 30 for management (10.30.30.10). CACHE01 runs LANCache: a caching proxy plus a DNS server that answers the game launchers' CDN hostnames with its own address. It's set up the same way: 10.30.10.20 in VLAN 10, 10.30.30.20 in VLAN 30.


  

    
ServerWhat matters for the network

    
      
BOOT01Reads, not writes, dominate: at opening time 40 seats each read a few GB within about two minutes. One 10G port on the same switch handles that at line rate. NVMe is the real limit, not the network.

      
CACHE0116 TB of disk is enough for the 40–60 titles a center actually runs. Its DNS forwards everything it doesn't cache to a filtering resolver upstream (step 6).

      
BothSpare 10G port sfp-sfpplus4 is the upgrade path: LACP the boot server when the room grows, or add a second cache.

    
  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • A seat with a temporary static address pings 10.30.10.10 and 10.30.10.20: the override ports work.
  • Seat-to-seat ping still fails.
  • Copy a 20 GB file from BOOT01 to a seat: ~110 MB/s, the seat's 1G port is the limit. Start the same copy on five seats at once: each still gets ~110 MB/s, because the server's 10G link isn't the bottleneck.

Here's what happens when a seat powers on:


  

    
  


  

    UEFI, legacy BIOS, and who answers PXE
    
ipxe.efi only works for UEFI firmware. Every gaming PC from the last decade boots UEFI, so one boot file is enough. In a mixed room you'd hand out different files depending on DHCP option 93 (client architecture), which RouterOS 7 can do with /ip dhcp-server matcher. Some diskless platforms prefer to answer the PXE part themselves as a proxyDHCP server. In that case, remove next-server and boot-file-name here, and let R1 hand out only the addresses.

10 · Step 6 of 12

  
The Game Cache & DNS

  
The cheapest bandwidth upgrade in the building: download each game once.


  
The seats' DNS server is CACHE01 (set in step 4). When Steam asks for its CDN hostname, the cache answers with its own address. The launcher then downloads from the cache at LAN speed. If the cache doesn't have the file yet, it fetches it once from the real CDN, stores it, and passes it on.


  

    
  


  
Every other DNS name is forwarded upstream to Cloudflare's malware-blocking resolver (1.1.1.2), or the family resolver (1.1.1.3) if the center admits under-16s on its own. To stop seats from bypassing the cache, and the filtering with it, with a hard-coded resolver, the forward chain drops DNS and DNS-over-TLS from seats. A seat's queries to CACHE01 never cross R1 (same VLAN), so the drop only hits bypass attempts:


  
R1 — seats use the cache's DNS, nothing else
/ip firewall filter
add chain=forward action=drop in-interface=v10-seats protocol=udp dst-port=53,853 \
    place-before=[find comment="seats -> internet"] comment="no DNS bypass"
add chain=forward action=drop in-interface=v10-seats protocol=tcp dst-port=53,853 \
    place-before=[find comment="seats -> internet"]


  

    Why not just redirect DNS with dst-nat?
    
The usual trick, dst-nat any port-53 traffic to the resolver you want, breaks here. The cache is in the same subnet as the seats, so its reply goes straight back to the seat at Layer 2, from an address the seat never asked. The seat drops it. That's the classic hairpin-NAT problem, and the drop rule avoids it entirely. DNS-over-HTTPS can't be blocked by port, so the seat image itself disables DoH in browsers and launchers by policy. That's one more advantage of a single golden image.

  


  

    What the cache can't do
    
LANCache works because the big launchers (Steam, Battle.net, Epic, Riot, PlayStation updates) still fetch game content over plain HTTP from predictable hostnames. Anything delivered only over HTTPS passes through uncached, because the cache can't read inside TLS and we're not going to break TLS on customers' machines. In practice the big downloads are cached and the long tail isn't.

WAN2: Failover & Where Downloads Go

  
The second ISP does two jobs: it carries the cache's downloads every day, and it carries everything on the day the fibre dies.


  
▲ Show step 8 in the interactive topology · see what this step adds, and click any device for its config


  
RouterOS detects a dead upstream with recursive routes. Each default route points at a public "probe" address (1.1.1.1 for WAN1, 9.9.9.9 for WAN2) that is itself only reachable through that WAN. check-gateway=ping pings the probe; when it stops answering, the default route that depends on it goes inactive and the next one takes over:


  

    
  


  
R1 — WAN2, recursive failover, and a routing table for the cache
! R1 — IP SLA + track instead of recursive routes, PBR instead of a routing table
ip sla 1

track 1 ip sla 1 reachability
ip sla 2

ip sla schedule 2 life forever start-time now
track 2 ip sla 2 reachability

ip route 9.9.9.9 255.255.255.255 198.51.100.1     ! VDSL modem in router mode, /30 to R1
ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 203.0.113.9 track 1
ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 198.51.100.1 250

/routing rule

add src-address=10.30.10.20/32 action=lookup table=via-wan2 comment="cache downloads ride WAN2"


  

    The rule order that keeps the cache on the LAN
    
The via-wan2 table only has default routes, no connected networks. Without the first rule, when CACHE01 answers a console in VLAN 20, that reply would match the cache's source-address rule, find the default route in via-wan2, and leave for the internet. The dst-address=10.30.0.0/16 → main rule has to come first. The same class of bug shows up in every policy-routing setup that forgets its own LAN. Also: 1.1.1.1 and 9.9.9.9 are now pinned to one WAN each, so never use either as a DNS server. That's why the resolvers are 1.1.1.2 and 1.0.0.2.

  


  

    What failover can't save
    
When WAN1 dies, every game in progress drops: the public IP changed, and no routing trick can move a live UDP session to a new address. Connections that were open on WAN1 also keep their old NAT mapping until they time out, which can take minutes. A /tool netwatch on 1.1.1.1 that runs /ip firewall connection remove [find reply-dst-address~"203.0.113.10"] when it goes down makes clients reconnect immediately. Failover keeps the room online; it doesn't keep the match alive.

  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • /ip route print where dst-address=0.0.0.0/0: "main: WAN1" active, "main: WAN2" standby.
  • Start a game download on a fresh title. /interface monitor-traffic pppoe-wan2 shows the cache pulling it in; WAN1 is quiet.
  • Unplug the fibre upstream of the ONT: within ~10–20 s seats browse via WAN2. Plug it back: they return to WAN1.
  • A console pings 10.30.10.20 (cache): works, proving the "LAN stays LAN" rule.

Latency First: The Queue Tree

  
Never let a download sit in front of a game packet. Separate the two classes, prioritise the small one, then share each class fairly per seat.


  

    
  


  
Telling "game" from "download" by port is hopeless: games use hundreds of ports and launchers use plain HTTPS. What reliably separates them is size. A TCP connection that has moved more than 5 MB is a download. Everything else (game UDP, voice chat, browsing, the session client) is interactive. The router marks connections once, marks packets by direction, and the queue tree does the rest:


  
R1 — mangle and queue tree (WAN1)
! R1 — NBAR2 classifies by application instead of by size
class-map match-any GAME
 match protocol attribute category gaming
 match protocol attribute category voice-and-video
class-map match-any BULK
 match protocol attribute category file-sharing
policy-map WAN1-CHILD
 class GAME
  priority percent 30
 class BULK
  bandwidth remaining percent 10
 class class-default
  fair-queue
policy-map WAN1-OUT
 class class-default
  shape average 190000000
  service-policy WAN1-CHILD
interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0
 service-policy output WAN1-OUT
! the same classes, shaped at 950M, go on the LAN-facing interface's output for downloads
! check the category names on your release: show ip nbar attribute category
/ip firewall mangle
add chain=forward action=mark-connection new-connection-mark=bulk protocol=tcp \
    connection-bytes=5000000-0 connection-mark=no-mark passthrough=yes comment="past 5 MB = a download"
add chain=forward action=mark-packet in-interface=ether1 connection-mark=bulk new-packet-mark=dl-bulk passthrough=no
add chain=forward action=mark-packet in-interface=ether1 new-packet-mark=dl-int passthrough=no
add chain=forward action=mark-packet out-interface=ether1 connection-mark=bulk new-packet-mark=ul-bulk passthrough=no
add chain=forward action=mark-packet out-interface=ether1 new-packet-mark=ul-int passthrough=no
/queue type
add name=pcq-down kind=pcq pcq-classifier=dst-address pcq-rate=0
add name=fq kind=fq-codel
/queue tree
add name=DOWN parent=global max-limit=950M
add name=down-int parent=DOWN packet-mark=dl-int priority=1 max-limit=950M queue=pcq-down
add name=down-bulk parent=DOWN packet-mark=dl-bulk priority=8 max-limit=950M queue=pcq-down
add name=UP parent=ether1 max-limit=190M
add name=up-int parent=UP packet-mark=ul-int priority=1 max-limit=190M queue=fq
add name=up-bulk parent=UP packet-mark=ul-bulk priority=8 max-limit=190M queue=fq


  

    Three things that silently disable this
    
1. FastTrack. Fast-tracked packets skip the queue tree. That's why decision 4 left it off. 2. Shaping at 100%. If max-limit equals the line rate, the queue forms in the ISP's equipment instead of here, and priorities never apply. Shape 5% below. 3. Per-seat fairness on upload. A queue on ether1 sees packets after masquerade, when every seat has the same source address, so a PCQ keyed on src-address would put the whole room into one bucket. Upload uses fq-codel (fair per flow) instead. Download is fine: the destination address was translated back to the seat before the queue sees it. Priority only works on child queues that have their own max-limit, which is why each one repeats it.

  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • Seat 31: start a large download that the cache doesn't handle (for example a 10 GB test file over HTTPS), so it runs through WAN1.
  • Seat 2: in-game ping to an EU server stays within 1–2 ms of its idle value, and a bufferbloat test (download + upload) scores A.
  • /queue tree print stats: down-bulk is dropping and queuing; down-int is not.

The Attacker's View

  
In a gaming center the attacker pays by the hour and sits next to the victim. Here's what's left for them to try.


  
    
    
  • Hardware keyloggers. A USB keylogger between keyboard and PC captures the next customer's game login. The network can't see it. Staff check the back of every seat at closing time, and keyboards with fixed cables plugged into a lockable back panel make it obvious.
    • 
    
  • Booting their own OS. A live USB on a seat bypasses the diskless image entirely. UEFI admin password, USB boot disabled, Secure Boot on. The network contribution is that even a foreign OS on a seat port is still isolated, still can't serve DHCP, and still only reaches the internet.
    • 
    
  • Attacks on the boot server. Every seat can reach BOOT01's iSCSI and TFTP ports, and that's unavoidable for diskless. Keep the platform patched, bind its admin UI to VLAN 30 only (staff reach it from the POS), and let iSCSI accept each seat's initiator only for its own overlay.
    • 
    
  • DDoSing a rival. Players leak each other's IPs through voice chat and peer-to-peer games, and booter services are cheap. That traffic lands on the center's shared WAN IP and lags everyone. Defences: an ISP plan with upstream DDoS filtering, no inbound port-forwards except UPnP on consoles, and WAN2 as an escape hatch.
    • 
    
  • Account and session theft. Customers forget to log out of Steam. The diskless reset handles it on reboot, and the session software should force a reboot at the end of each paid session, not just a logout.
    •

    What actually changes in the design
    
Classification by application instead of by size. RouterOS calls any TCP connection past 5 MB "bulk"; Cisco NBAR2 recognises the game and download applications themselves. That's more precise, but it depends on NBAR2 knowing the titles your center runs, and the download direction has to be shaped on the LAN side, because the WAN policy only sees upload. No UPnP means open NAT for consoles needs public addresses. Protected ports aren't override lists: an unprotected port reaches every protected one, so tournament mode also moves the bracket to its own VLAN.
Cost is the real difference. Roughly ten times the network budget for the same result. Cisco makes sense here when the center is part of a chain that already runs Cisco and manages it centrally.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Diskless seats make R1 (clean PCs) a solved problem, as long as the boot server sits in the seats' Layer 2 domain so the boot storm never reaches the router.
    2. 
      
  2. Customer-to-customer protection belongs in the switch chip. Port isolation blocks seat-to-seat at line rate, in hardware, with one subnet and no VLAN sprawl.
    3. 
      
  3. A LAN cache plus DNS is the biggest bandwidth upgrade per euro, and blocking DNS bypass keeps it (and its filtering) in the path.
    4. 
      
  4. Pin games to one WAN. Use the second for bulk and for failure. Recursive routes catch the upstream faults that a link light misses, and the LAN-first routing rule prevents the classic policy-routing leak.
    5. 
      
  5. Queue by size, not by port. Connections past 5 MB are downloads; everything else is interactive. Shape below line rate, keep FastTrack off, and use fq-codel where NAT hides the seats.
    6. 
      
  6. Tournament mode is a port list, not a redesign. One scheduled script flips the bracket's isolation and logs that it did.
    7. 
      
  7. The threat sits at the keyboard. UPnP only for consoles, snooping on every switch, BIOS locks, and a reboot at the end of every session.
