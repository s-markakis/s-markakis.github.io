This is part 5 of the Build series. The law firm had confidential files; a clinic has health data, which GDPR treats as a special category with stricter requirements. The supermarket had payment devices it couldn't trust; a clinic has medical devices it can't update, because their software is certified as part of the device. And unlike either, a clinic's network failing has a direct effect on patient care.

    
The design answer is the same pattern applied more strictly: put each category of device in its own small network, give each network exactly the paths it needs, keep the clinical work local so it doesn't depend on the internet, and make the internet itself redundant for the parts that live in the cloud.

The Brief

  
A fictional but typical private outpatient clinic: GPs and specialists, imaging on site, 20 staff, 150 patients a day.


  
The clinic has six consulting rooms, each with a PC and a desk phone, plus a treatment room where doctors use tablets. Reception has three desks that run the appointment system and the electronic health record (EHR), both cloud services. Imaging is a digital X-ray (detector plus acquisition workstation) and an ultrasound system, both sending images to a PACS (the image archive and viewer). The lab has two blood analyzers that send results to a LIS (lab information system) bridge, which forwards them to the EHR. There are six cameras, three printers, hosted VoIP, and a waiting room where patients expect Wi-Fi. One of the doctors is on call some nights and needs to look at images from home.


  

    
RequirementWhere it comes from, and what it forces

    
      
R1 Health dataGDPR Art. 9 and 32: health data needs protection appropriate to the risk. Clinical systems are segmented from everything patients and visitors can reach, and access is logged.

02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Each of these trades a little convenience for something a clinic can't do without.


  
1 · Medical devices get their own switch

  
The X-ray, ultrasound and lab analyzers sit on a separate CRS326 with a 10G uplink, together with the PACS and NAS. Imaging studies are large (a CT-sized study can be hundreds of MB, even plain X-ray sets add up), so the 10G path between devices, router and PACS matters. Physically separating the device switch also means the people who service medical equipment can work on their switch without touching the one patients' and staff devices hang off.


  
2 · The PACS is local, the archive is in the cloud

  
Images go from the modality to the on-site PACS over DICOM, and doctors view them from there. A nightly encrypted copy goes to a cloud archive for retention and disaster recovery. The clinic's daily work never waits on the internet, and the archive copy means a burnt-out server closet doesn't lose years of patient images.


  
3 · Per-device Wi-Fi keys instead of 802.1X

  
802.1X with a RADIUS server is the textbook answer for staff Wi-Fi, but it needs a server and a certificate setup that a 20-person clinic won't maintain. RouterOS 7's wifi access list can give each MAC address its own passphrase on the same SSID (sometimes called PPSK). A lost tablet's entry is deleted in seconds, and every other device keeps working.


  
4 · Vendors dial out, on a schedule

  
Imaging vendors usually ask for "a port forward to the workstation". That would put an unpatchable Windows machine on the internet. Instead, the vendor's support agent on the workstation is allowed to connect out to the vendor's hub, and only while a scheduled firewall rule is enabled. No inbound port exists, ever.

    Medical devices and "don't touch the network settings"
    
Medical device vendors usually forbid changes on the device itself, including adding software, changing its firewall, sometimes even its IP address. That's why every control here lives in the network: static addressing set once by the vendor's engineer, a port that accepts only that device's MAC, and (step 5) a firewall that lets it reach only its server. Agree the addressing with the vendor before installation day; changing a modality's IP later can mean a paid service visit.

add ssid-regexp="^CLINIC-STAFF\$" mac-address=AA:BB:CC:00:10:02 passphrase="*****************" action=accept comment="tablet 02"

/interface wifi provisioning add action=create-dynamic-enabled master-configuration=cfg-patients \
    slave-configurations=cfg-staff
# patients: 10 Mb/s each, 100/20 in total
/queue type
add name=pcq-pt-down kind=pcq pcq-classifier=dst-address pcq-rate=10M

Tablets need a fixed MAC on this SSID (turn off MAC randomization for it in the device management profile), otherwise the access-list entry won't match. Per-device keys need WPA2-PSK. On SW-CLINIC, ether20–22 become AP trunks: management VLAN 99 untagged, VLANs 10 and 50 tagged.

  • A laptop that knows tablet 01's key but has a different MAC is rejected.
  • Delete tablet 02's entry: tablet 02 drops off; tablet 01 is unaffected.

    Which DICOM port?
    
The DICOM standard port is 104, but many PACS products listen on 11112 instead (104 is a privileged port on Linux), and some modalities use a second port for the worklist. Take the AE titles, IPs and ports from the PACS vendor's install sheet and write each one as its own rule from the specific modality's address. Watch /log print where message~"FWD-DROP" during the first study: a DICOM echo that times out almost always means a port the install sheet didn't mention.

}
add name=vx-close start-date=2026-11-04 start-time=17:00:00 interval=7d on-event={
  /ip firewall filter disable [find comment=VENDOR-XRAY]
  /ip firewall connection remove [find src-address~"^10.60.30.11:"]
  :log warning "vendor window CLOSED (X-ray)"
}


  
Closing the window takes two actions: disabling the rule stops new connections, and removing the workstation's tracked connections ends the one already open (otherwise the established/related accept would keep an engineer's session alive past 17:00). For an emergency call-out, the practice manager asks the IT contractor to run vx-open by hand, and the warning log lines on the monitoring box record when it happened.

  • Wednesday 14:00: the agent shows "online"; the vendor can start a session.
  • 17:00: the session is cut, and the log has both window events.

Backups & Hardening

  
Patient images and records must survive ransomware, theft and fire. The network's part is making sure the backups are unreachable from everything a user touches.

PACS + LIS databasesThe NAS pulls them hourly from VLAN 35 with a read-only service account; it then pushes a nightly encrypted copy to an immutable cloud bucket.

      
Router + switch configsEach device runs a nightly /export and uploads it to the NAS with /tool fetch over SFTP. The devices start the connection, so the NAS never needs a path into the MGMT VLAN.

No clinical, reception, patient or medical-device VLAN has any rule allowing traffic to the NAS (10.60.35.20). The NAS reaches out; nothing reaches in except management. Residual Risk

  
What the network can't fix on its own, and what covers each gap.


  
    
    
  • Old operating systems on medical devices. They will stay old until the vendor certifies an update. The network limits each device to its server and its vendor window; the clinic's job is to keep a register of device versions and ask vendors for their patch plans at every service visit.
    • 
    
  • Accounts, not networks, protect the EHR. The EHR is a cloud service reachable from any internet connection. Strong individual logins with MFA, and the EHR's own access logs, are the real control there.
    • 
    
  • Phishing on clinical PCs. A PC that's compromised by email can still reach what its user can reach: the PACS viewer, the LIS, the EHR. Endpoint protection, least-privilege Windows accounts and email filtering cover that; the network keeps it away from the devices, the servers' file shares and the backups.
    • 
    
  • Unattended screens. Consulting rooms are left open between patients. Short screen-lock timeouts and badge or PIN unlock matter as much as any VLAN.
    • 
    
  • The server closet. Lock it, keep it cool, and put the PACS, NAS, router and switches on a UPS sized for a clean shutdown.
    •

The Attacker's View

  
I test clinics like this for a living. Here's what I'd try, and why the network turns each attempt into a small, bounded problem.


  
    
    
  • Start from the waiting-room Wi-Fi. The patient VLAN forwards to the internet only; a scan from a chair finds the gateway and nothing clinical — no PACS, no LIS, no EHR bridge, no cameras.
    • 
    
  • Own the old imaging workstation. The X-ray acquisition PC runs an OS the vendor froze years ago — my easiest shell in the building. But VLAN 30 lets each medical device reach only its own server and its vendor's update window, so owning the box buys no path to the EHR, the file shares or the backups.
    • 
    
  • Ride the telemedicine tunnel home. The on-call doctor reads images from home over WireGuard, so I'd target the home device instead. The per-doctor key lands only on the PACS-viewer subnet, not the full clinical VLAN, and it is revoked per person.
    • 
    
  • Pass-back the scan-to-folder printer. Point the MFP's LDAP/SMB "test" at my laptop to catch its bind account. The printer's admin UI is reachable from VLAN 99 only, staff get raw print on 9100, and the scan account can write to its one share and nowhere else.
    • 
    
  • Commit toll fraud on the VoIP. The voice VLAN reaches the hosted PBX and nothing else; there is no route from a phone to a clinical host and no inbound dial-through to abuse.

    What actually changes in the design
    
Per-device Wi-Fi keys need a server on Cisco. Identity PSK works the same way for the user, but the controller asks a RADIUS server for each tablet's key, which is the server this design avoided. The vendor window is simpler: a time-range on a stateless ACL ends the session at closing time without any flush. Medical ports use "restrict" so an unknown device is logged and dropped without taking a modality offline.
The rest translates one to one. The cost difference is about five to six times for the network hardware.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Fence unpatchable medical devices into their own VLAN, reaching only their server on its documented ports, never the internet. Every control lives in the network because the device itself can't be changed.
    2. 
      
  2. Keep imaging local. A PACS on site means X-rays reach doctors in seconds with or without internet; the cloud gets an encrypted archive copy overnight.
    3. 
      
  3. Per-device Wi-Fi keys give most of 802.1X's benefit (revoke one device without touching the rest) without a RADIUS server, as long as each entry is scoped to its SSID.
    4. 
      
  4. Reserve the backup line for patient care. Reject patient Wi-Fi and archive uploads on 5G, at the top of the chain.
    5. 
      
  5. Vendors dial out on a schedule. A disabled rule, two scheduler entries and a connection flush replace the port forward vendors ask for.
    6. 
      
  6. Give on-call access one door. A WireGuard peer per laptop, reaching the PACS viewer and nothing else, with the viewer's own MFA behind it.
