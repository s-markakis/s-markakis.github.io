Build: The
Supermarket Network

One store in a regional chain: eight staffed lanes, four self-checkouts, a warehouse full of handheld scanners, shelf labels and deli scales that change price overnight, cold rooms that must never warm up, and 24 cameras. The tills talk to head office and nowhere else. We build it on Cisco from the lanes up in 12 tested steps, ending with an IKEv2 tunnel to HQ, an LTE backup reserved for what keeps the store trading, and a refrigeration vendor who can only get in on Tuesday mornings.

This is part 4 of the Build series, and the first one that's part of something bigger: the store is a branch of a chain. The POS backend, ERP, price management and security operations all live at head office. The store's network has to do two things well: keep its own zones apart, and give each zone exactly the path to HQ it needs.

    
Retail is also where payment-card rules bite hardest. With dozens of payment devices in one building, every zone that can reach a till is in PCI DSS scope. The design aim is that almost nothing can.

01

  
The Brief

  
A fictional but typical 1,500 m² neighbourhood supermarket, store number 142 in a chain of 60.


  
The store has 8 staffed lanes, each with a POS terminal and an IP PIN pad, plus 4 self-checkouts. The sales floor uses electronic shelf labels driven by 6 radio base stations, the deli, bakery and butcher have networked scales, and screens at the entrance show offers. The warehouse uses Wi-Fi handheld scanners for receiving and stock counts. The cold rooms and display fridges are monitored by a refrigeration controller that the refrigeration contractor services remotely. There are 24 cameras, a PA and music system, a small office, and customer Wi-Fi. Head office runs the POS backend, payment gateway, ERP, warehouse management and price systems in its data center, reached over the internet.


  

    
RequirementWhere it comes from, and what it forces

    
      
R1 Payment scopePCI DSS: the lanes and self-checkouts form the cardholder data environment (CDE). They reach HQ's POS and payment systems and nothing else; nothing in the store reaches them.

      
R2 Keep tradingA store that can't take cards loses thousands an hour. Lanes and handhelds must survive a fibre cut (LTE backup), and the LTE line carries nothing else.

      
R3 HQ is the only way out for the CDEThe tills never browse; payments go through HQ's gateway. Store-to-HQ traffic is encrypted end to end.

      
R4 Prices change overnightHQ pushes the next day's prices to scales and shelf labels between 02:00 and 05:00. That path has to exist, in that direction only.

      
R5 Food safetyRefrigeration alarms reach HQ's monitoring 24/7. The contractor gets in only in agreed windows, from one known host, and every session is logged.

      
R6 Warehouse roamingHandhelds move between 4 APs while scanning a pallet; roams must be fast enough that the scanning app doesn't time out.

      
R7 CCTV24 cameras at up to 4 Mb/s each stay off every other switch, and off the WAN except for HQ security viewing.

      
R8 Chain-wide samenessSixty stores, one design. Addresses, VLAN numbers and configs are templates where only the store number changes.

    
  


  
  
02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Most of these come from the store being one of sixty.


  
1 · The CDE has no internet route at all

  
The lanes don't need "internet with a filter"; they need HQ. So the CDE's ACL on the core permits only HQ's address space, and the zone firewall on the edge has no INSIDE→OUTSIDE class for the CDE subnet. A compromised till can't phone home to anyone but head office. Proving that, which a PCI assessor will ask for, is a two-line check rather than a review of an internet filter.


  
2 · CCTV gets its own switch

  
Twenty-four cameras would fill an access switch's ports, PoE budget and uplink. A dedicated C9200L-48P with an uplink that carries only VLAN 60 keeps camera traffic and any compromised camera physically away from the lanes, and lets the camera installer work on that switch without touching anything else.


  
3 · A route-based tunnel to HQ

  
A static virtual tunnel interface (IKEv2 + IPsec, tunnel mode) makes HQ look like a routed next hop: ip route 172.16.0.0 255.255.0.0 Tunnel0. That is easier to template across 60 stores than crypto maps, plays well with QoS, and allows a second tunnel over LTE with a floating route. The tunnel is also its own firewall zone, so "what may HQ reach in the store" is a zone-pair policy.


  
4 · The LTE line is tunnel-only

  
During a fibre outage, the store doesn't need the internet. It needs HQ. The LTE interface is in its own zone that allows nothing but the backup tunnel's IKE and ESP. The office, guests and signage simply wait, so the LTE data plan stays small and there's nothing to filter.

    Why a PIN pad port must not recover by itself
    
PCI DSS expects stores to detect swapped or tampered payment devices. With maximum 1 and a sticky MAC, a different device on a lane port shuts the port immediately, and with errdisable recovery off it stays shut. That's noisy on purpose: the lane stops taking cards, a supervisor gets called, and someone physically inspects the PIN pad before IT re-enables the port. The sticky MACs are saved into the startup config with write memory after the store's devices are installed, and a PIN pad replacement is a ticket, not a shrug.

  


  

    Checkpoint: test this before building the next step
    
  • show port-security: one sticky MAC per lane port after all lanes are powered.
  • Swap lane 3's PIN pad for a spare: Gi1/0/5 goes err-disabled and a syslog message appears; it stays down until shutdown/no shutdown after the sticky entry is cleared.
  • Lane 1 and lane 2 POS (static 10.50.10.11 and .12) ping each other: same VLAN, no routing yet.

    Fast Transition and old scanners
    
Some older handheld scanners fail to associate at all with an SSID that advertises 802.11r. Test the actual scanner model before the store opens. If it's a problem, use "FT adaptive" mode (FT for clients that ask for it, plain WPA2 for the rest) or a separate SSID for the old devices until they're replaced. A personal (PSK) key on a scanner fleet is a known trade-off: rotate it when a scanner is lost, and the HANDHELD VLAN's ACL in step 6 limits what a stolen key can reach.

policy-map WAN-CHILD

  bandwidth percent 30

  fair-queue

  service-policy WAN-CHILD

# R1 — WireGuard re-encapsulates, so queue the tunnel interface itself
/ip firewall mangle add chain=forward action=mark-packet new-packet-mark=crit \
    out-interface-list=HQ \
    src-address-list=CRITICAL passthrough=no
/ip firewall address-list
add list=CRITICAL address=10.50.10.0/24
add list=CRITICAL address=10.50.30.0/24
/queue tree
add name=hq parent=wg-hq1 max-limit=60M
add name=hq-crit parent=hq packet-mark=crit priority=1 limit-at=20M max-limit=60M
add name=hq-rest parent=hq packet-mark=no-mark priority=8 max-limit=60M


  
qos pre-classify matters here: after encryption, every packet leaving Gi0/0/0 to HQ is ESP between two public addresses, and the ACL above would never match. With pre-classify, the egress policy classifies on the original inner headers.

The Attacker's View

  
Where the residual risk sits once the segmentation is in place, and what covers it.


  
    
    
  • Payment-device tampering. The biggest retail risk is physical: overlays and swapped PIN pads. The network makes a swap noisy (sticky MAC, no auto-recovery), but daily visual inspection of each PIN pad against its serial number is what catches an overlay.
    • 
    
  • Network sockets in public areas. Signage, self-checkouts and lane cabinets sit where customers stand. Every port has a fixed VLAN, unused ports are shut and parked, and the CDE ports accept only their one known device.
    • 
    
  • Third-party access. Retail breaches often start with a contractor's access. Here no vendor connects to the store directly: one jump host at HQ, one address, one port, one window, all logged.
    • 
    
  • IoT with long lives. Scales, shelf-label base stations and refrigeration controllers run old firmware for years. Their VLANs can reach their managers and (for IoT) HTTPS out, never the CDE or the office.
    • 
    
  • Head office itself. A compromise at HQ shouldn't spread to every store. The HQ→INSIDE zone policy limits HQ to specific management functions per VLAN rather than full access.
    •

    What actually changes in the design
    
The tunnel technology changes, and with sixty stores that's a head-office decision, not a store one: HQ's head-ends must speak WireGuard (or IPsec with GRE). A swapped PIN pad is silent on MikroTik: the port just stops forwarding, with no err-disable state and no log line. For PCI evidence, add a monitoring check on the bridge host table or DHCP leases that alerts on a new MAC in VLAN 10. Routing moves to R1 and the firewall becomes stateful, as in the other MikroTik builds.
Fleet management is the other real difference: at sixty stores, Cisco shops use central tooling; MikroTik shops template RouterOS configs and push them with the API or Ansible.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Give the CDE exactly one destination. Lanes reach HQ and nothing else, enforced on the core, in NAT and in the zone firewall, so no single mistake opens an internet path for a till.
    2. 
      
  2. Make payment-device swaps loud. Sticky MAC with no automatic recovery turns a tampered PIN pad into a stopped lane and a phone call.
    3. 
      
  3. Route-based tunnels template well. A static VTI with a route to HQ, certificates from the chain's CA, and a dynamic VTI at the head-end scale to every store.
    4. 
      
  4. A backup link can be tunnel-only. Two tunnels, two head-ends, each pinned to its own path, plus a floating route: the failover needs no new IKE negotiation and the LTE line carries nothing else.
    5. 
      
  5. Don't trust head office blindly. HQ→store is a zone policy with specific functions, and vendors come through one jump host inside a time window.
    6. 
      
  6. Classify before you encrypt. qos pre-classify is what lets the WAN policy see a card payment inside the tunnel.
