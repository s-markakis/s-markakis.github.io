This is part of the Build series, and the one that leaves the building. A farm network is not one site — it's a shed with power, a block of greenhouses a few hundred metres away, and sensors scattered across a grove where there is no power, no cable and no Wi-Fi that would ever reach. Three different radio problems, one router, and a link to the outside world that is a dish on a pole.

    
The design leans on three ideas that none of the earlier builds needed: use the right radio for the distance — LoRa for the grove, long-range Wi-Fi for the greenhouses, Starlink for the world; treat power as a budget, not a given; and keep the things that act — pumps, vents, valves — locked to exactly what they need, because an irrigation controller on the internet is a pump someone else can turn on.

01

  
The Brief

  
A fictional but very typical Cretan operation: 30 ha of olives, a block of six greenhouses, a packing shed, and an agronomist who visits once a week.


  
The packing shed has the only mains power for a kilometre (grid plus a generator), a small office with two PCs, a scale and label printer for crates, and an NVR for the yard cameras. A block of six greenhouses sits about 600 m away: each has a climate controller (vents, heating, humidity), a fertigation/irrigation controller, and a tablet the grower uses. Across the olive grove, dozens of battery soil-moisture and weather probes report every fifteen minutes. One far corner of the grove needs a camera on a track gate where no cable and no power reach. Internet is Starlink on the shed roof. The agronomist and the operator both work remotely.


  

    
RequirementWhere it comes from, and what it forces

    
      
R1 Sensors across a groveDozens of probes over 30 ha, each running years on a battery. Wi-Fi can't do range or battery life; this is what LoRaWAN exists for.

      
R2 Greenhouses 600 m awayToo far for a cable, too much to trench. A long-range 5 GHz bridge carries the control VLAN across the gap.

      
R3 Controllers that actPumps, vents and valves can cause real damage. The controllers must reach their own vendor cloud and nothing else, and nothing may reach them except the office dashboards.

R6 Remote everythingStarlink and LTE are both behind carrier NAT, and nobody is on site. Management and monitoring dial out over a tunnel.

      
R7 Power is not a givenGrid cuts in summer storms, a generator that runs dry, a battery that sees a week of cloud. Every node has to degrade and recover on its own.

    
  


  
  
02

  
Four Design Decisions

  
Each is a choice about which radio, and about what a device that controls water is allowed to talk to.


  
1 · The right radio for the distance

  
There are three wireless problems here and they are not the same problem. The grove needs kilometres of range and years of battery from tiny messages a few times an hour — that is LoRaWAN, not Wi-Fi. The greenhouses need a real VLAN trunk across 600 m of line of sight — that is a 5 GHz point-to-point bridge. The world needs bandwidth — that is Starlink. Using Wi-Fi for the grove, or LoRa for the greenhouses, would fail in opposite directions.


  
2 · Sensors have no IP; the gateway does

  
A LoRaWAN sensor never joins the IP network. It transmits an encrypted frame into the air; the gateway hears it and forwards the raw packet to a network server (here, The Things Stack), which decrypts it and hands the reading to the farm's dashboard. So the grove adds one device to the network — the gateway — on its own small VLAN, and the hundreds of possible sensors add nothing to attack on the wire.


  
3 · Things that act are locked down hardest

  
An office PC leaking is a bad day. An irrigation controller reachable from the internet is a pump that floods a field or a greenhouse that cooks its crop. The control VLAN may reach only its vendor's cloud (DNS-pinned), and only the office dashboards may reach it. No sensor, no camera, no guest ever touches it.


  
4 · Power is a line item

  
The shed has mains; everything else has a budget. The solar node's draw (router + camera + LoRa), times the hours of darkness and a margin for cloudy days, sets the battery; the battery and the worst-month sun set the panel. Get it wrong and the gate camera dies every January. We size it explicitly in step 7, the way you'd size a queue.

! No Cisco box is a drop-in for a MikroTik wAP LR8. Cisco's LoRaWAN story is the
! IXM gateway + IoT Field Network Director, aimed at utilities and smart cities —
! heavy and licensed for a six-greenhouse farm. In practice you run ANY vendor's

/system identity set name=LORA-GW
# ether1 is the trunk from SW-SHED
/interface vlan add name=v35-lns interface=ether1 vlan-id=35
/interface vlan add name=v99-mgmt interface=ether1 vlan-id=99

# Semtech UDP packet-forwarder to The Things Stack (EU868)
/iot lora servers add name=tts address=eu1.cloud.thethings.network up-port=1700 down-port=1700
/iot lora set lora0 servers=tts network=public antenna-gain=3


  
LoRaWAN splits the job in two. The sensor wakes a few times an hour, sends one small encrypted frame, and sleeps — which is how a coin cell lasts years. The gateway is a dumb, powerful ear: it hears every frame in range and forwards it, untouched, to the network server, which owns the keys and decrypts it. That is why the gateway sits alone on VLAN 35 with a single job — reach The Things Stack on UDP 1700 — and why adding a hundred more probes across the grove changes nothing on the wire.


  

    LoRa is a duty-cycle, not a pipe
    
EU868 is legally limited to roughly 1% airtime per device. LoRaWAN is for small, infrequent readings — soil moisture, a tank level, a gate contact — not images or streaming. The farm camera in step 7 rides LTE for exactly this reason; only its sensor data would go over LoRa.

  


  

    Checkpoint

  • A test probe joins (OTAA) and its uplinks appear in the console with a sane RSSI/SNR.
  • From the gateway, /ip route print resolves and it can reach only VLAN 35's gateway — not the office.

Long-Range Wi-Fi to the Greenhouses

  
600 m of line of sight, one VLAN trunk, no trench. A pair of 5 GHz dishes and MikroTik's nv2 protocol.

  
▲ Show step 4 in the interactive topology · select SXT pair for its config


  # SXT-SHED (AP end). nv2 = MikroTik TDMA: scheduled slots beat 802.11 back-off over distance
  
SXT pair — 5 GHz nv2 bridge carrying the control VLAN
! There is no nv2 on Cisco. For a 600 m point-to-point link Cisco's answer is the
! Catalyst IW (Industrial Wireless) or Ultra-Reliable Wireless Backhaul line — they
! do PtP well but cost far more than a pair of SXTs. What you give up dropping to
! plain 802.11 is nv2's TDMA scheduling: the trick that keeps a long, one-to-one
! link stable without CSMA back-off waiting for a faint, distant station. The VLAN
! trunk it carries (control VLAN 30 + mgmt) is identical whatever bridges the gap.
/interface wireless set wlan1 mode=ap-bridge band=5ghz-a/n/ac channel-width=20mhz frequency=5500 \
    ssid=farm-ptp wireless-protocol=nv2 nv2-security=enabled nv2-preshared-key="********" distance=1
# radio joins the bridge → the VLAN trunk rides the air
/interface bridge port add bridge=bridge interface=wlan1
# far end: same radio in station-bridge, its ether1 feeds a small switch
# controllers sit on V30 at the far end — untagged on their ports, tagged across the link


  
A point-to-point link is a cable you can't trench. The two SXT dishes are aimed at each other across the grove; nv2, MikroTik's TDMA protocol, replaces 802.11's listen-before-talk with scheduled time slots, which is what makes a long, one-to-one link stable where plain Wi-Fi would stall waiting for a faint, distant station. The link is a transparent bridge, so VLAN 30 (and management) crosses it exactly as if the greenhouses were in the shed — the controllers never know they are 600 m and one radio hop away.


  

    Line of sight is a maintenance item
    
5 GHz needs a clear Fresnel zone. An olive branch or a new polytunnel in the path drops the link from gigabit-class to nothing. It goes on the service-visit checklist next to the dish alignment — the same way the villa build checks its 60 GHz bridge for a growing tree.

  


  

    Checkpoint
    
  • /interface wireless monitor wlan1 at each end: connected, signal better than −65 dBm, nv2.
  • A controller at the greenhouse gets 10.60.30.x and its dashboard loads from an OPS PC.
  • From STAFF, the controller does not answer — only OPS crosses into VLAN 30.

The forward chain reads as the policy matrix, line for line. OPS reaches the controllers, the cameras and the internet. CONTROL and LNS and CCTV each reach only the internet (to be narrowed to their vendor by DNS address-lists). STAFF and GUEST get the internet and nothing of the farm's. Everything else falls to the logged drop at the bottom. The single most important line is that last drop: without it, a compromised greenhouse PLC could scan straight into the office.


  

    Egress, not just ingress
    
It is tempting to only stop things reaching the controllers. But a controller that can reach the whole internet is a foothold with an outbound channel. Pinning CONTROL and CCTV egress to their vendors' addresses (an address-list fed by DNS, as in the supermarket build) turns a compromised device from a pivot into a dead end.

  • From an OPS PC, the controller dashboard and the camera feeds both load.
  • The FWD-DROP log shows attempts, so you can see what the design is turning away.

This is the villa's Starlink pattern, and it earns its place again: in bypass mode the dish stops routing and R1 takes the carrier-NAT address directly, so the farm's firewall — not Starlink's box — is the edge. The recursive default route pings 1.1.1.1 through Starlink's gateway; a DHCP-client script rewrites that gateway whenever it changes, so check-gateway can tell a real outage from a reboot. The 4G line sits at distance 2 and only carries traffic when the dish is down — out here, that means a summer storm or the dish icing in the rare winter one.


  

    Checkpoint
    
  • /ip route print where comment~"probe" shows the same gateway as /ip dhcp-client print.
  • Unplug the dish: within seconds the route flips to 4G and the office stays online.
  • Sensor uplinks keep arriving at The Things Stack throughout — LoRa traffic is tiny and survives the failover.

A camera and a LoRa repeater on a gate with no power and no cable. The hard part isn't the config — it's the power budget.

Solar node — LtAP LR8 LTE · camera + LoRa repeater
! No single Cisco box is a solar-powered LoRa repeater + LTE camera node. The
! closest is an IR1101 industrial router: DIN-rail, wide-temperature, built-in LTE,
! low draw — but it has no LoRa concentrator, so a third-party gateway still rides
! alongside it. The hard part isn't the vendor anyway: the power budget (load x dark

/system routerboard settings set cpu-frequency=auto

/interface wireguard add name=wg-node listen-port=13232

    endpoint-port=51820 allowed-address=10.250.0.0/24 persistent-keepalive=25s

/iot lora servers add name=tts address=eu1.cloud.thethings.network up-port=1700 down-port=1700
/iot lora set lora0 servers=tts network=public

# watchdog reboots it on a wedge — nobody's driving out
/system watchdog set watchdog-timer=yes


  
The node backhauls over its own LTE and dials a WireGuard tunnel home, so the camera reaches the shed NVR and the LoRa concentrator reaches the same network server as the shed gateway — all without a single cable leaving the gate. The config is deliberately frugal: the CPU clocks down, logging is trimmed to spare the flash, and the hardware watchdog reboots the board if a brownout wedges it, because the nearest pair of hands is 30 minutes away.


  

    Sizing the battery, the way you'd size a queue
    
Add the loads: router + LTE ≈ 4 W, camera ≈ 3 W, concentrator ≈ 1 W → ~8 W, about 190 Wh/day. For four days of autonomy through cloud, at 50% usable depth of discharge, you need ≈ 190 × 4 ÷ 0.5 ≈ 1,500 Wh of battery — a 12 V / 130 Ah LiFePO4. The panel has to refill a day's draw in the worst month's few good hours: 190 Wh ÷ ~3.5 winter sun-hours ÷ 0.8 losses ≈ 70 W, so fit a 120 W panel and an MPPT controller for margin. Undersize either and the gate goes dark every January.

  


  

    Checkpoint
    
  • The WireGuard peer shows a recent handshake; the camera appears in the NVR.
  • The node's LoRa gateway shows connected in The Things Stack alongside the shed one.
  • Pull the panel at dusk: the battery monitor projects more than four days of runtime.

Residual Risk

  
What the network can't do on its own, and what covers each gap.


  
    
    
  • The vendor clouds. Controllers and cameras depend on their makers' clouds and the accounts that log into them. The network keeps the devices isolated; the vendors' account security — the grower's MFA, strong passwords — protects the dashboards themselves.
    • 
    
  • LoRaWAN keys. A sensor's security is its join keys. A probe pulled from a fence post and read out is a compromised sensor, not a compromised network — but it should be deactivated in The Things Stack, and keys should never be the factory default.
    • 
    
  • Physical access in a field. Boxes on poles and in greenhouses are reachable. Lockable enclosures, disabled unused ports, and management on its own VLAN mean a plugged-in laptop lands nowhere.
    • 
    
  • The generator and the battery. The network rides through cuts, but the diesel still has to be filled and the LiFePO4 bank replaced at end of life. Both belong on the service-visit checklist.
    • 
    
  • The operator hub. It holds a key to every site the operator runs. It gets MFA, a dedicated machine, and a separate WireGuard key per farm so one can be revoked alone.
    •

The Attacker's View

  
I test networks like this for a living. On a farm the prize is the things that move water and power — here's what I'd try, and why each stays bounded.


  
    
    
  • Turn a pump or a vent on. The controllers are the real target — flood a field, cook a greenhouse. But VLAN 30 is reachable only from OPS and egresses only to its vendor; from staff Wi-Fi, a guest, a sensor or a camera, the controllers simply don't answer, and the controller itself can't be driven from the open internet.
    • 
    
  • Pull a sensor off a fence post. A probe is physically exposed, so I'd read its keys and spoof readings. That compromises one sensor's data, not the network: the probe has no IP and no path inward, and a bad node is deactivated at the network server. The dashboard owner's job is to notice readings that don't make sense.
    • 
    
  • Jam the greenhouse link. A burst on 5 GHz drops the PtP and the greenhouses go blind. It's a denial-of-service, not a break-in — the controllers keep their last program and fail safe locally, and the link uses nv2 with a key so I can't simply associate to it. Line of sight and a spare radio are the recovery, not a firewall rule.
    • 
    
  • Own the off-grid node. The solar node is alone in a field on LTE — tempting. But it only holds a WireGuard key scoped to the hub's /24 and a camera stream; it can't reach the control VLAN, and its key is revocable on its own. Draining its battery by keeping its radio busy is the more realistic attack, and the watchdog plus the power budget are what absorb that.
    • 
    
  • Ride a WAN uplink home. Both WANs are CGNAT with no inbound, and management only exists inside the dial-out tunnel. There is no listening service on the public side to reach — the farm is only ever a client of the internet, never a server on it.
    •

Appendix

  
The Same Design on Cisco

  
Most of this maps cleanly; two parts don't, and it's worth knowing which.


  
The shed router and switch become a Catalyst and an IR1101 industrial router (DIN-rail, wide temperature, built-in LTE) — the firewall is a zone-based policy, the VLANs and inter-VLAN routing are the same idea with different words. WireGuard becomes an IKEv2/FlexVPN tunnel dialled out to a hub, and the failover is IP SLA plus a tracked route instead of a recursive one.


  

    What doesn't map
    
LoRaWAN. Cisco's answer is its own IXM gateway and IoT platform, aimed at utilities and cities, not a six-greenhouse farm. In practice you run a third-party gateway to The Things Stack either way — the router just carries its UDP traffic.

    
The long-range bridge. There is no nv2. Cisco's Industrial Wireless / Catalyst IW radios do 802.11-based point-to-point well, but the TDMA-over-distance trick that makes a cheap SXT pair reliable at 600 m is MikroTik's. For one farm, the MikroTik radios are the right call; Cisco earns its place when a cooperative standardises across many sites.

  


  

    
Takeaways

    
    
      
  1. Match the radio to the distance. LoRa for sparse, battery-years sensors; 5 GHz nv2 for a VLAN trunk across a field; Starlink for the world.
    2. 
      
  2. Sensors are RF endpoints, not hosts. Only the gateway touches the wire, on its own small VLAN with one job — reach the network server.
    3. 
      
  3. Lock the things that act hardest. A controller reaches its vendor and nothing else; only the office reaches it; pin egress, not just ingress.
    4. 
      
  4. Power is a budget. Load × dark hours × a cloud margin sizes the battery; a day's draw over the worst month's sun sizes the panel.
    5. 
      
  5. Everything dials out and recovers itself. Carrier NAT on both WANs, a watchdog on the lonely node, and a config that comes back after a cut without a drive into the hills.
